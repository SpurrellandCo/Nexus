# Patterns — New Functional Training for Sports (Michael Boyle)

Concrete techniques, progressions, and design patterns from the book, organized for reuse.

## The Three Questions Test (Ch 1)
**When to use**: Evaluating whether an exercise or program design is "functional."
**How**: Ask (1) how many sports are played sitting down? (2) how many sports are played with stability provided by an outside source? (3) how many sports skills are performed by one joint in isolation? Answer is "almost none" to all three — so default exercises to standing, self-stabilized, multijoint.
**Trade-offs**: Some isolation work (deep abdominals, hip abductors, scapular stabilizers) is still needed for "innervation" — don't apply the test dogmatically to those three groups.

## Joint-by-Joint Diagnosis (Ch 5)
**When to use**: An athlete has pain at a joint and you need to find the real cause.
**How**: Map joints bottom-up: ankle (mobility) → knee (stability) → hip (mobility, sometimes also stability) → lumbar spine (stability) → thoracic spine (mobility) → scapula (stability) → glenohumeral (mobility, trained for stability). Check the joint immediately above and below the pain site before treating the painful joint itself.
**Trade-offs**: Requires the coach to resist the instinct to treat the site of pain directly.

## Squatting to Powerlifting Parallel (Ch 6)
**When to use**: Teaching or coaching any knee-dominant squat pattern.
**How**: Define depth by femur-parallel-to-floor, not knee angle. Use a 12-inch box + Airex pads as a depth gauge. Never coach half/quarter squats. Never coach below-parallel squats for general athletes.
**Trade-offs**: Requires more coaching precision than a generic "squat to 90 degrees" cue, but avoids both under-depth (ankle-dominant compensation) and over-depth (ligament/meniscus risk) failure modes.

## Baseline / Progression / Regression System (Ch 4, applied throughout)
**When to use**: Selecting and advancing any exercise in any program.
**How**: Classify every exercise as Baseline (default starting point, ~3 weeks), Progression (numbered, harder), or Regression (numbered, easier). Progress on the "eye test" (does it look smooth and athletic?), not on a calendar. Regress immediately on technical struggle or injury history.
**Trade-offs**: Requires a coach able to judge movement quality in real time — the whole system depends on "watching workouts," not just writing them.

## Push-Pull Ratio Check (Ch 8)
**When to use**: Auditing an athlete's shoulder-injury risk from strength imbalance.
**How**: Estimate chin-up 1RM (bodyweight + added load, via an RM conversion table) and compare to bench press 1RM. The chin-up-equivalent number should be ≥ the bench number. If bench significantly exceeds it, flag elevated shoulder-injury risk.
**Trade-offs**: A screening heuristic, not a diagnosis — still requires normal technique assessment on both lifts.

## Mini-Band Walks + Cross-Body Reaching (Ch 6)
**When to use**: An athlete has anterior knee pain or struggles with unsupported single-leg squats despite doing fine on split squats.
**How**: Mini-band walks (band at ankles or feet, maintain tension, step without rocking) and cross-body reaching (reach the arm across the working leg during hip/knee-dominant exercises) to activate the gluteus medius as a stabilizer.
**Trade-offs**: Treats the hip as the root cause of "knee valgus" rather than treating the knee directly — requires re-education if the athlete/coach is used to quad-focused rehab.

## The 4-Phase Plyometric Progression (Ch 9)
**When to use**: Introducing or advancing jump training for any athlete, any level.
**How**: Phase 1 (minimum 3 weeks) — stable-landing jumps/hops/bounds, developing eccentric strength ("tendon training"). Phases 2–3 — build jumping skill and introduce elasticity. Phase 4 — true plyometrics (minimized ground-contact time). Gate progression on competence (quiet landing, matched takeoff/landing depth), not time elapsed.
**Trade-offs**: Slower than jumping straight to "real" plyometrics, but the source of nearly all plyometric injuries is skipping Phase 1.

## Hang-Position-Only Olympic Lift Teaching (Ch 10)
**When to use**: Teaching clean/snatch variations to athletic (non-competitive-weightlifting) populations.
**How**: Never lift from the floor. Follow the 4-step positional sequence: hands-free front squat → clean-grip front squat → start position (Pendlay technique: bend knees, don't hinge chest over bar) → overhead support position.
**Trade-offs**: Sacrifices some "purity" relative to competitive weightlifting technique, but dramatically lowers technical difficulty and back-injury risk for a general athletic population.

## The Tri-Set / Density Model (Ch 11)
**When to use**: Structuring any training session for maximum "work per hour."
**How**: Explosive/power work first. Pair primary strength exercises with core or mobility work to fill rest intervals (a tri-set). Run remaining accessory work as another tri-set or mini-circuit (2–3 sets, ≤1 min rest).
**Trade-offs**: Requires more session planning than straight-set programming, but converts idle rest time into productive volume.

## Tempo Running → Shuttle Running Conditioning Base (Ch 2, 11)
**When to use**: Building a conditioning base for sprint-dominant sport athletes without long steady-state work.
**How**: Start with 6–8 min/week of tempo striding (30–100 yd stride, 30–40 yd walk recovery), building to ~1,000 total yards. Progress to shuttle runs on a 25–50 yd course, initially reducing total distance (~750 yd) to offset the higher stress of direction changes, then increasing 10–20%/week.
**Trade-offs**: Slower to build a subjective "in shape" feeling than long jogging, but avoids the motor-pattern and tissue-quality costs of steady-state distance work.

## High-Low Conditioning Distribution (Charlie Francis, Ch 11)
**When to use**: Structuring the weekly split between hard and easy conditioning days.
**How**: Every conditioning session is either >90% effort or <80% effort — nothing in between. One hard day per week, two or three ~70%-effort days (tempo, slide board, bike).
**Trade-offs**: Requires discipline not to "round up" moderate-intensity days into hard days, which is the exact failure mode it's designed to prevent.

## Nonbouncing vs. Bouncing Medicine Ball Selection (Ch 7)
**When to use**: Choosing medicine ball type for a given throw pattern.
**How**: Use nonbouncing balls for rotational throws and slams (matches striking's single-effort, non-repeated nature). Use bouncing rubber balls only for overhead throws (where the rhythmic catch-recatch has real training value).
**Trade-offs**: Requires stocking two ball types rather than one, but each is matched to the actual skill demand rather than convention.
