# Glossary — New Functional Training for Sports (Michael Boyle)

**Acceleration** — the 0–10 yard, 0-to-top-speed quality that actually determines team-sport performance; distinct from top-end speed, which is rarely reached in sport (Ch 5).

**Amortization phase** — the ground-contact/shock-absorption period between landing and the next takeoff in a jump; true plyometrics aim to minimize it (Ch 9).

**Ankle-dominant squatting** — a compensation pattern where weak or injured athletes drive the knees forward over the toes to lower center of gravity, increasing knee stress; fixed with heel elevation (Ch 6).

**Antiextension** — the primary function of the anterior core muscles: resisting spinal extension, not producing spinal flexion (Ch 7).

**Antilateral flexion** — the core function of resisting lateral bending of the trunk, trained via side planks and variations (Ch 7).

**Antirotation** — arguably the most important core function: resisting rotation of the trunk relative to the pelvis; trained via chop/lift patterns, never via loaded trunk twists (Ch 7).

**Baseline exercise** — the general starting-point exercise for the average athlete in Boyle's classification system; held ~3 weeks before progressing (Ch 4).

**Bilateral limb deficit (BLD)** — the phenomenon where summed unilateral force output exceeds bilateral force output; the physiological basis for prioritizing single-leg training (Ch 6).

**Bound** — a plyometric action: single-leg takeoff, landing on the opposite foot (Ch 9).

**Chainsaw principle** — Boyle's analogy for Olympic lifting: a powerful tool that helps when used skillfully and hurts when used carelessly (Ch 10).

**Closed-chain** — the foot (or working limb) is in contact with the ground/a stable platform, allowing coordinated multi-muscle function; near-synonymous with "functional" (Ch 1).

**Closed-pack position (90-45)** — the shoulder joint angle (90° abduction, elbow at 45°) where the glenohumeral joint surfaces align best; the correct position for rotator cuff isolation work (Ch 8).

**Cook/Sous-Chef/Chef model** — Boyle's proficiency framework for how much a coach should deviate from an established program template (Ch 4).

**Core (broad definition)** — all muscles of the body's midsection: rectus abdominis, transversus abdominis, multifidus, internal/external obliques, quadratus lumborum, spinal erectors, and (partly) glutes/hamstrings/hip rotators (Ch 7).

**Creep** — the property of soft tissue to elongate and stiffen under sustained low load (e.g., prolonged sitting); the physiological rationale for foam rolling (Ch 5).

**Deadlift (hip-dominant movement)** — per Dan John & Pavel Tsatsouline, a movement with "deep hip movement and minimal knee bend" (Ch 6).

**Eccentric strength** — the ability to absorb and decelerate a moving load; Boyle's #2 reason for Olympic lifting and central to plyometric Phase 1 and injury prevention broadly (Ch 9, 10).

**Foam rolling / self-myofascial release** — self-massage using a foam roller to decrease muscle tissue density before stretching (Ch 5).

**Functional anatomy** — the model (developed by Gary Gray) describing how muscle groups act together across joints during ground contact, replacing "origin-insertion" isolated-joint anatomy (Ch 1).

**Functional continuum** — a taxonomy rating exercises from least to most functional based on stability source, stance, and joint count (Ch 4).

**Functional strength** — strength an athlete can actually use in sport-relevant, closed-chain, often single-leg positions, as opposed to strength expressed only in artificial positions (Ch 3).

**Gluteus medius** — the primary lateral hip stabilizer for single-leg movement; weakness here is often misdiagnosed as a knee problem (Ch 6).

**Gravity-enhanced / gravity-reduced** — Boyle's plyometric intensity classification: jumping over an obstacle (gravity-enhanced) vs. jumping up onto a box (gravity-reduced) (Ch 9).

**Hang position** — the Olympic-lift starting position with the bar above the knees; Boyle's exclusive teaching position, eliminating floor-pull technique and back stress (Ch 10).

**High-low conditioning model** (Charlie Francis) — structure conditioning sessions at either >90% or <80% effort, never in between (Ch 11).

**Hip hinge** — moving from the hip with zero lumbar spine flexion and minimal knee bend; the defining motion of the single-leg straight-leg deadlift (Ch 6).

**Hop** — a plyometric action: single-leg takeoff, landing on the same foot (Ch 9).

**Isolation for innervation** (Mark Verstegen) — using brief, isolated exercise to "turn on" key stabilizer muscle groups (deep abdominals, hip abductors/rotators, scapular stabilizers) before they function within compound movement (Ch 1).

**Joint-by-joint approach** (Gray Cook & Michael Boyle) — the model that the body's joints alternate between needing mobility and needing stability, and that dysfunction at one joint shows up as pain at the joint above or below (Ch 5).

**Jump** — a plyometric action: double-leg takeoff, double-leg landing (Ch 9).

**Kipping** — using body momentum to complete a chin-up/pull-up rep; banned as cheating in Boyle's system (Ch 3, 8).

**Knee-dominant movement (squat)** — per Dan John & Pavel Tsatsouline, a movement with "deep movement of the knees AND hips" (Ch 6).

**Locked long / locked short** (Thomas Myers) — tissue states resulting from creep; posterior-chain tissue tends to become "locked long" from sitting (Ch 5).

**Medial vs. lateral hops** — hopping toward the body's midline (medial) is more difficult and provides more hip-stabilizer stress than lateral hops (Ch 9).

**Mobility vs. flexibility** — flexibility targets muscles and requires static holds; mobility targets joints and requires gentle motion (Ch 5).

**Open-chain** — the working limb is not in contact with the ground/a stable platform; near-synonymous with "nonfunctional" for lower-extremity training (Ch 1).

**Powerlifting parallel** — Boyle's squat-depth standard: the femurs parallel to the floor, avoiding both partial-ROM squats and "full" below-parallel squats for general athletes (Ch 6).

**Progressive resistance** — adding load or reps nearly every week as the primary strength-development engine (Ch 4).

**Progression / Regression** — numbered exercise variations easier (regression) or harder (progression) than the baseline, used to individualize difficulty (Ch 4).

**Proprioception** — internal sensory feedback about body position and movement; degraded by externally-stabilized (machine-based) training (Ch 1).

**Push-pull ratio** — the relationship between an athlete's pulling strength (chin-up 1RM + bodyweight) and pressing strength (bench 1RM); should be ≥1:1 to protect the shoulder (Ch 8).

**Sled marching / sled running (special vs. specific strength)** — heavy sled marching (special strength) vs. lighter sled sprinting (specific strength), used to bridge strength training and sprint mechanics (Ch 5).

**Special strength** — resistance movements incorporating the joint dynamics of a skill without fully imitating it (e.g., sled marching) (Ch 5).

**Specific strength** — resistance movements directly imitating a skill's joint action (e.g., sled running) (Ch 5).

**Sports-general training** — training built around movement commonalities shared across most sports (speed, core, push/pull/hip patterns), as opposed to sport-specific skill mimicry (Ch 1).

**Technical failure** — the point at which an athlete can no longer maintain perfect exercise technique; used as the stopping criterion for RM-based strength testing (Ch 3).

**Tempo running** — runs at a "stride" pace (between jog and sprint) with walking recovery, used for base-building and conditioning (Ch 2, 11).

**Three questions test** — Boyle's diagnostic for functional training: do few sports involve sitting, externally-stabilized, or single-joint movement? (Ch 1).

**Train slow, get slow** — the principle that endurance-style training degrades speed and power qualities faster than speed/power training builds them (Ch 2).

**Tri-set** — Boyle's session-density technique: pairing two strength exercises with a core or mobility exercise filling the rest interval between them (Ch 11).

**VO2 (maximal oxygen uptake)** — a steady-state aerobic capacity measure historically misapplied to speed/power athletes (Ch 2).
