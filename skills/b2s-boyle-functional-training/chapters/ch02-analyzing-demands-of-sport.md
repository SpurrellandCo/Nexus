# Chapter 2: Analyzing the Demands of the Sport

## Core Idea
Before designing a program, analyze what actually makes top performers dominant in a given sport — almost always speed, power, and the ability to repeatedly accelerate/decelerate — rather than defaulting to generic fitness testing (especially aerobic capacity) borrowed from exercise physiology.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Sport Analysis Questions**: A structured set of questions to determine training priorities for any sport.
  - When to use: At the start of any program design process, before choosing exercises or conditioning methods.
  - How: Ask — Does the sport require sprinting or jumping? Do you stop and start frequently? How long is a play/event, and how long is the rest between efforts? Are athletes on the field the whole time? If they jog, is it ever for more than 5 minutes (if not, why train it)? Does the athlete's speed/power rank in the top 10% of their sport (see benchmark table below)?
- **"Train Slow, Get Slow"**: The principle that endurance-style training degrades speed and power qualities, and it is far easier to make an athlete slow than fast.
  - When to use: Deciding whether to include long, slow distance conditioning (e.g., cross country) for speed/power-sport athletes.
  - How: Avoid steady-state distance training for team/power-sport athletes; replace with sport-matched interval work and strength training. Reserve endurance emphasis for genuine endurance sports only.
- **Analyze Strengths, Not Just Weaknesses** (via Charlie Francis): Programs should be built around amplifying the qualities that make top performers dominant (speed, power) rather than reflexively "fixing" whatever an athlete tests poorly on (e.g., aerobic capacity).
  - When to use: Interpreting fitness test results, especially when an explosive athlete scores poorly on an aerobic capacity (VO2) test.
  - How: Recognize that poor VO2 performance in a fast-twitch-dominant athlete is expected and not a flaw to correct; correcting it by adding endurance work can actively convert power-oriented (white/fast-twitch) muscle fiber toward endurance (red) fiber, which is undesirable for a speed/power athlete.

## Key Concepts
- **Speed and power sports** — the category most team sports and many individual sports (gymnastics, figure skating, tennis) fall into; dominant performers are the most efficient, explosive movers, not the most flexible or aerobically fit.
- **Endurance sports** — the minority category (e.g., distance running, rowing over distance) where steady-state aerobic capacity is the limiting quality.
- **VO2 (maximal oxygen uptake)** — a steady-state aerobic capacity measure historically misapplied to speed/power athletes by exercise physiologists using generic test-analyze-conclude protocols.
- **Fast-twitch (white/power-related) muscle fiber** vs. **red/endurance muscle fiber** — Charlie Francis's model of trainable fiber-type shifts; power training during ages 13–17 helps preserve genetically-determined fast-twitch levels and shift transitional fiber toward power; excessive endurance work shifts transitional fiber toward endurance.
- **Sport-specific training (conditioning sense)** — matching a sport's actual work-to-rest ratio and effort pattern (e.g., football: ~5-second plays, ~30–40-second rests) rather than mimicking total distance covered.

## Mental Models
- Watch the game, watch the great players, and look for common denominators — don't focus on what elite athletes can't do; figure out why they do what they do well.
- Ask "at what speed and over what time period?" whenever someone justifies aerobic training with total distance covered in a game (e.g., "a soccer player runs 5 miles") — 5 miles in 2 hours is a 2.5 mph walking pace, not a training-relevant intensity.
- It takes years to build speed and power but only weeks to build aerobic conditioning — sequence training priorities accordingly, and treat endurance work as an afterthought for power-sport athletes.

## Anti-patterns
- **Generic "test → analyze → conclude" protocols borrowed from exercise physiology**: Applying steady-state VO2 testing (often on a cycle ergometer) to speed/power athletes who don't train that way, then "fixing" the resulting poor score with aerobic training.
- **Long, slow distance training (e.g., cross country) for team/power-sport athletes**: Fails to prepare athletes for the actual demands of their sport (repeated short sprints with rest), has a roughly 60% injury rate among new runners, and can measurably reduce explosive power (see Zoe Hickel worked example).
- **Treating "recovery benefits from aerobic training" as justification for endurance volume**: Defenders point to aerobic training's role in recovery, but this is a different goal from improving performance — the chapter's focus is performance, where aerobic emphasis is the wrong lever for explosive athletes.
- **Cross-training as a primary method rather than limited active recovery**: Cyclists should ride, rowers should row, sprinters should sprint on the ground — cross-training in high volumes displaces the sport-specific adaptations that matter.

## Reference Tables
**Speed/power benchmark thresholds (top 10% indicator, per Boyle):**

| Metric | Male threshold | Female threshold |
|---|---|---|
| 10-yard dash (electronic timing) | under 1.65 sec | under 1.85 sec |
| Vertical jump | over 34 in (86 cm) | over 25 in (64 cm) |

If an athlete doesn't clear these numbers, more speed and power development is always warranted.

**Illustrative work:rest demands by sport (from the text):**

| Sport | Typical effort pattern |
|---|---|
| Football | ~5-second plays, ~30–40 seconds rest between plays |
| Soccer | Series of sprints interspersed with jogs/walks over ~2 hours; not continuous running |
| Hockey | Short series of sprints, several minutes rest, repeat (shift-based) |
| Tennis | Repeated sprint/decelerate efforts over a match lasting up to 2 hours, not continuous motion |

## Worked Example
**Zoe Hickel (University of Minnesota Duluth women's hockey captain) — regaining lost power:**
At 18, Zoe was a top recruit and U.S. under-18 national team member, but three years of college training — heavy on endurance work, no meaningful lower-body strength emphasis — had *decreased* her vertical jump. In 2014 she trained for a summer at Mike Boyle Strength and Conditioning under a program with little to no endurance training (longest run of the summer: a 300-yard shuttle). Changes made:
- Significant reduction in endurance work
- Increased emphasis on lower-body strength
- Program designed specifically to add lean mass

Result after 7 weeks: vertical jump increased 3 inches (back to her level from 3 years prior), she gained 6 lb, was the leading scorer at her first national tryout camp in 4 years, earned a U.S. women's national team spot, and had her best-ever collegiate offensive season (19 goals, 13 assists, All-League honors). This is Boyle's concrete case for "train slow, get slow" and its reversal.

## Key Takeaways
1. Analyze a sport by its actual work:rest structure and effort intensity, not by total distance or duration.
2. For the large majority of sports (speed/power sports), endurance training is a secondary consideration, not the foundation.
3. "Train slow, get slow" — long, slow distance work degrades explosive qualities and carries high injury risk (~60% of new runners get injured).
4. Build programs around amplifying what makes top performers dominant (speed, power, efficient movement), not around "fixing" poor scores on generic fitness tests.
5. Power-related training in the 13–17 age range helps preserve fast-twitch fiber potential — a developmental window worth protecting from excessive endurance volume.
6. Use the 10-yard dash and vertical jump benchmarks as a quick top-10%-of-sport screen for whether an athlete needs more speed/power work (nearly everyone does).
