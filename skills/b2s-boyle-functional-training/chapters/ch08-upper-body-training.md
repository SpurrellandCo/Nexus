# Chapter 8: Upper Body Training

## Core Idea
Upper body training must deliberately balance pulling and pushing volume 1:1 — because athletes and coaches gravitate toward "mirror muscle" pressing work (chest, arms) and neglect pulling (chin-ups, rows), and that imbalance is a primary driver of rotator cuff and shoulder injuries, not the pressing exercises themselves.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Push-Pull Ratio Test**: A direct, individualized way to check for pulling/pushing imbalance.
  - When to use: Auditing whether an athlete's program (or an athlete's actual strength) is balanced enough to protect the shoulder.
  - How: Estimate 1RM chin-up by adding chin-up-max-added-weight to bodyweight (e.g., a 200 lb athlete doing 45 lb added for 5 reps → 245 lb total → use a 5RM-to-1RM conversion table → ~280 lb estimated 1RM chin-up). This number should be **equal to or greater than** the athlete's bench press 1RM. If bench press significantly exceeds the chin-up-equivalent number, the athlete is at elevated risk of shoulder injury from push-pull imbalance.
- **Dan John's Strength Standards, Boyle's Amendment**: A cross-lift consistency check.
  - When to use: Verifying an athlete is training all qualities (not just their favorite lift) with equal effort.
  - How: Original standard: Bench = front squat = clean. Boyle's amendment (since front squats were dropped): **Bench = split squat = clean = chin-up** (all at matched RM, e.g., 5RM). If an athlete can bench 300 lb but can't split-squat 120 lb dumbbells for 8 or clean 265 lb for 5, "he isn't trying hard enough" in those other lifts — this applies to female athletes too (e.g., 135 lb bench should pair with a 135 lb hang clean and 55 lb dumbbell split squats for 8). High school football reference standards: clean 205 lb, bench 205 lb, squat 255 lb, clean & jerk 165 lb.
- **Minimum Weekly Pulling Volume Rule**: A concrete programming floor.
  - When to use: Writing any upper body program.
  - How: At least 3 sets of a chin-up variation per week, plus a minimum of 3 sets of 2 different rowing movements per week — one set of pulling for every set of pushing. Vary the specific vertical/horizontal pull exercise, the rep scheme, or both, every 3 weeks to keep progressing.

## Key Concepts
- **Vertical pulling** — chin-up/pull-up family (supinated grip = chin-up, easiest due to biceps assistance; pronated/parallel-grip variations are harder) and pull-down variations (used as an assisted regression, not a chin-up substitute).
- **Horizontal pulling** — dumbbell rows, suspension trainer inverted rows, single-arm/single-leg rows (static and dynamic hip variants), single-arm double-leg rotational rows.
- **Scapulothoracic joint** — the shoulder blade–rib cage articulation; needs dedicated stabilization training (Y/T/W positions) because a strong rotator cuff still needs a stable scapular base to work from — "strengthening the rotator cuff without strengthening the scapular stabilizers does only half the job."
- **Closed-pack position (90-45)** — the joint angle (90° abduction, elbow out at 45°) where the glenohumeral joint surfaces align best; the correct position for rotator cuff isolation work, replacing the old nonfunctional "elbow at side, bent 90°" standing/side-lying position.
- **Kipping** — using body momentum to complete a chin-up/pull-up rep; explicitly banned ("the only thing kipping is good for is ego inflation").

## Mental Models
- Athletes chase "mirror muscles" (chest, arms) because of "muscle magazine education," not because it's the right training priority — actively counter this bias by defaulting to equal or greater pulling volume.
- "If you are going to stink at one lift, stink at the bench. It's the least important" — reframes the bench press's cultural primacy; prioritize chin-up, hang clean, and split squat parity over bench numbers.
- Don't fight to eliminate sacred-cow exercises like the bench press — incorporate more functional exercises alongside it and let athletes discover through experience (e.g., failing at an inverted row) that they lack the torso stability functional training addresses.
- Move the arms *by moving the scapulothoracic joint*, not the reverse — this single coaching cue converts a "shoulder exercise" into a true scapular-stabilizing exercise.

## Anti-patterns
- **Lat pulldowns as a substitute for chin-ups/pull-ups, rather than a stepping-stone to them**: Most athletes default to pulldowns "under the mistaken assumption that this is all that is necessary," which under-trains true vertical pulling strength.
- **Programs emphasizing pressing (bench, overhead press) with little to no equivalent pulling volume**: The single biggest driver of rotator cuff overuse injury per Boyle — "due less to the bench press itself and more to the lack of an equivalent number of pulling exercises."
- **Kipping to complete chin-up/pull-up reps**: Cheats the strength stimulus and inflates ego numbers without building real vertical pulling strength.
- **Rotator cuff work done in the old "arm at side, elbow bent 90°" standing/side-lying position**: An extremely nonfunctional joint position that muscles never actually work in; replace with the 90-45 closed-pack position.
- **Treating all scapular/shoulder isolation work as inherently "nonfunctional" and skipping it entirely**: The either/or framing (multijoint-only vs. isolation) misses that scapulothoracic and glenohumeral joints specifically benefit from targeted stability work — same "functional paradox" logic as Ch 1.
- **Letting weak scapular retractors substitute lat adduction for scapular retraction in the T position**: A common compensation (pulling the arms down slightly, using lats instead of retractors) that Boyle says must be guarded against — the shoulder angle should never drop below 90° in the T position.

## Reference Tables
**Weekly upper body pulling/pushing minimums:**

| Movement category | Minimum weekly volume |
|---|---|
| Chin-up variation (vertical pull) | ≥3 sets/week |
| Rowing movements (horizontal pull) | ≥3 sets/week across 2 different row variations |
| Pushing (press) | Matched 1:1 in sets against total pulling volume |

**Vertical pulling progression:**

| Stage | Exercise |
|---|---|
| Progression 1A (easiest) | Chin-up (supinated grip, 12–14 in / 30–36 cm width) |
| Progression 1B–2 | Parallel-grip pull-up |
| Progression 3 | Pull-up → sternum chin-up |
| Regression 1 | Pull-down variations (X pull-down, alternating X pull-down) — used when bodyweight vertical pulling isn't yet achievable |
| Assistance method | Heavy-duty resistance band looped over the bar; knee or foot in band, work down from heavy → medium → light band → full bodyweight |

**Chin-up progression volume (first 8 weeks):** 3×8–10, then 3–5×5, then 3–5×3; don't introduce exercise variety yet — beginners need less variety than advanced trainees.

**Scapulothoracic Y/T/W position guide (AT Sports Flex or cable):**

| Position | Arm angle | Cue |
|---|---|---|
| Y | 45° above shoulder level, thumbs up | External rotation emphasis |
| T | 90° to torso, thumbs up | Scapular retraction — angle must never drop below 90° (guards against lat substitution) |
| W | 45° to torso | Scapular retraction emphasis |

Sports Flex High-Low (Y-W combo): 8 reps each side, no rest between switching sides. Sports Flex T: add 2 reps/week up to 12/position (36 total), then shift to a maintenance program.

**Rotator cuff (standing external rotation):** performed in the 90-45 closed-pack position; high reps, 15–20 range, because these muscles function as stabilizers, not force producers.

## Worked Example
**Push-pull ratio worked calculation (from the text):** A 200 lb athlete performs a chin-up with 45 lb added for 5 reps → total load 245 lb → using the RM conversion table, this projects to a ~280 lb 1RM chin-up-equivalent. This athlete's bench press 1RM should be at or below 280 lb. If his bench press significantly exceeds 280 lb, Boyle flags him as at elevated risk of shoulder injury purely from the push-pull imbalance, independent of technique on either individual lift. The same logic scales down for female athletes: a 135 lb (61 kg) bench should pair with a 135 lb hang clean and 55 lb (25 kg) dumbbell split squats for 8 reps (110 lb total per Table 8.1, projecting to a 135 lb 1RM).

## Key Takeaways
1. Program at least 1 set of pulling for every set of pushing — never let pressing volume outpace pulling volume.
2. Use the push-pull ratio test (chin-up-equivalent 1RM ≥ bench press 1RM) to flag individual athletes at elevated shoulder-injury risk.
3. Apply Dan John's amended standard (bench = split squat = clean = chin-up, matched RM) to catch athletes who are undertraining lifts outside their comfort zone.
4. Ban kipping — it's ego inflation, not strength development.
5. Scapulothoracic stability work (Y/T/W positions) is necessary even though it looks like isolation training — a strong rotator cuff still needs a stable scapular base.
6. Coach rotator cuff work in the 90-45 closed-pack position at high reps (15–20), not the old bent-elbow-at-side position.
7. Don't try to eliminate the bench press culturally — add functional pulling and pressing variations alongside it and let results build buy-in gradually.
