# Chapter 6: Lower Body Training

## Core Idea
Lower body strength training should be built primarily around unilateral (single-leg) exercises — split squats and single-leg straight-leg deadlifts above all — because they reduce back injuries, transfer better to the single-leg nature of running and jumping, and (via the bilateral limb deficit) let athletes handle more relative load safely than heavy bilateral squats.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Knee-Dominant vs. Hip-Dominant Classification** (via Dan John & Pavel Tsatsouline's *Easy Strength*): Replaces the outdated "squat vs. deadlift" naming, which breaks down once you have goblet squats, kettlebell sumo deadlifts, and rear-foot-elevated split squats that don't fit neatly into either bucket.
  - When to use: Categorizing any lower-body exercise and deciding what it emphasizes.
  - How: A **deadlift/hip-dominant** movement has "deep hip movement with minimal knee bend" (e.g., kettlebell swing, single-leg straight-leg deadlift). A **squat/knee-dominant** movement has "deep movement of the knees AND hips" (e.g., goblet squat, single-leg squat). Classify by movement pattern, not by where the weight is held.
- **Squat Pattern Before Squat Load**: "Adding strength to dysfunction" (Gray Cook) applied specifically to the squat.
  - When to use: Deciding whether an athlete is ready to load a squat variation.
  - How: Teach the body-weight squat pattern (mobility) as part of warm-up, outside the weight room, removing the ego/psychological pressure to add load. Do the majority of loaded squat-pattern strength work through unilateral exercises instead of loading the bilateral squat directly. Only add external load once the pattern is mastered.
- **Squatting to "Powerlifting Parallel"**: A depth standard reconciling coach and therapist concerns.
  - When to use: Teaching and coaching squat depth for any knee-dominant exercise.
  - How: Define depth by the femur being parallel to the floor (not by a 90° knee angle — a common therapist error). Never coach half or quarter squats (they concentrate more risk under heavier loads). Never coach below-parallel/"full" squats for general athletes (per Carl Klein's *The Knee in Sports* — full flexion overstretches the collateral and cruciate ligaments and risks a posterior meniscus tear); reserve below-parallel squatting for competitive weightlifters whose sport requires it.
- **The Bilateral Limb Deficit (BLD)**: The physiological basis for prioritizing unilateral training.
  - When to use: Justifying why single-leg loading can safely exceed "half of the two-leg number," and why unilateral training builds real strength efficiently.
  - How: Per Kuruganti, Murphy, and Pardy (2010), the summed unilateral force output of two limbs working alone exceeds their combined bilateral output — an athlete is often able to rear-foot-elevated split squat with more than half of what they back squat on two legs (sometimes matching their front squat load 1:1 in a split squat).
- **Mini-Band Walks and Cross-Body Reaching**: Two techniques to "turn on" the lateral glutes (gluteus medius) when an athlete struggles with true single-leg squats.
  - When to use: An athlete does fine on split squats/single-leg bench squats but struggles with unsupported single-leg squats, or has anterior knee pain (chondromalacia, patellar tendinitis, patellofemoral syndrome) traced to weak hip stabilization rather than weak quads.
  - How: Mini-band walks — band at ankles (or on feet for more hip external rotation), maintain band tension, step laterally without rocking side to side. Cross-body reaching — reach the arm across the working leg during a knee- or hip-dominant exercise to increase pelvic rotation against the fixed femur, stretching and activating the glutes as external rotators. Reframe "knee valgus" (knee caving in) as a hip adduction/internal rotation problem, not a knee problem.

## Key Concepts
- **Hip hinge** — moving from the hip with zero lumbar spine flexion, knees bent only 10–20°, all remaining motion from the hip (the "golfer's lift"); the defining motion of the single-leg straight-leg deadlift.
- **Squat pattern (vs. "the squat")** — Boyle deliberately teaches the *pattern* (mobility, positioning) separately from loading a specific squat variation; the goal of early squat work is mobility assessment, not load.
- **Ankle-dominant squatting** — a compensation pattern where weaker athletes or those returning from injury drive the knees forward over the toes to lower their center of gravity, avoiding quad demand but increasing knee flexion and stress; corrected with heel elevation (a 1×4 in / 2.5×10 cm board or wedge) to provide "artificial ankle mobility."
- **Gluteus medius** — the primary lateral hip stabilizer for single-leg movement (running, jumping, squatting); when weak or neurologically "not turned on," knee structures (IT band, patellar tendon, kneecap) compensate and take on pain.
- **Posterior chain** — glutes, hamstrings, long adductors; chronically neglected relative to knee-dominant/squat-pattern work in conventional programs, despite hamstring strains being far more common in sport than quad strains.

## Mental Models
- Think of the squat as revealing information, not just building strength — a body-weight or goblet squat exposes ankle mobility, hip mobility, and hamstring flexibility deficits before any load is added.
- "Who cares?" about precise squat-vs-deadlift semantics — what matters is whether the pattern is hip-dominant (minimal knee bend) or knee-dominant (shared hip/knee bend), because that's what determines training emphasis.
- If you can only program two lower-body exercises, make them the split squat and the single-leg straight-leg deadlift — Boyle's default starting point for any beginner.
- You rarely hear about a "pulled quadriceps" in sports, but pulled hamstrings are common — let that asymmetry guide programming emphasis toward hip-dominant, posterior-chain work.

## Anti-patterns
- **Loading a squat pattern the athlete hasn't mastered ("adding strength to dysfunction")**: The likely root of many athletes' back pain, per Boyle's own observation that "almost all our athletes' back pain issues resulted from performing heavy squats."
- **Coaching squat depth by knee angle (e.g., "squat to 90 degrees") instead of femur-parallel-to-floor**: A common therapist/trainer error that produces inconsistent, often too-shallow squats.
- **Half squats and quarter squats**: Explicitly banned in Boyle's programs — partial-ROM squats invite heavier loads and a correspondingly greater risk of back injury.
- **Below-"powerlifting-parallel" ("full") squatting for general athletes**: Per Klein's research, risks overstretching knee ligaments and tearing the posterior meniscus; reserve for competitive weightlifters whose sport demands it.
- **Step-ups as a primary single-leg substitute for the squat**: Lacks the initial eccentric contraction of a true single-leg squat and is too easy to cheat by pushing off the ground leg — not Boyle's preferred single-leg movement.
- **Treating knee valgus ("knee caving in") as a knee problem**: It's actually hip adduction and internal rotation — fix it with hip stabilizer work (mini-band walks, cross-body reaching), not knee-focused intervention.
- **Overemphasizing squat/knee-dominant work while neglecting hip-dominant posterior-chain exercises**: Leaves the glutes and hamstrings undertrained relative to injury risk (hamstring strains are common; quad strains are rare).

## Worked Example
**Diagnosing and fixing anterior knee pain via hip stabilization, not knee work:**
An athlete performs split squats and single-leg bench squats fine but struggles with true single-leg squats, and reports chondromalacia patellae, patellar tendinitis, or patellofemoral pain. The old model blamed weak quadriceps and prescribed leg extensions (a nonfunctional, open-chain exercise — see Ch 1). Boyle's model instead diagnoses a weak or under-activated gluteus medius that's failing to stabilize the femur in single-leg stance, forcing the knee's passive structures (IT band, patellar tendon) to compensate and become painful. Fix: mini-band walks (band at ankles, maintain tension, step without rocking — or band on the feet for more external-rotation stimulus) and cross-body reaching added into existing single-leg exercises. This reclassifies what looks like a "knee problem" as a hip stability problem and treats the actual cause.

## Key Takeaways
1. Classify exercises by movement pattern (knee-dominant = deep hip AND knee movement; hip-dominant = deep hip, minimal knee movement), not by the old squat/deadlift naming.
2. Teach the squat pattern (mobility) separately from loading it — do most heavy lower-body loading through unilateral variations instead.
3. Coach squat depth to "powerlifting parallel" (femur parallel to floor), never half/quarter squats, and generally not below parallel for general athletes.
4. The bilateral limb deficit means single-leg loading can safely and legitimately exceed half of an athlete's bilateral squat number.
5. If an athlete has anterior knee pain and struggles with true single-leg squats, suspect a weak gluteus medius (hip stability), not weak quads.
6. Default to split squats + single-leg straight-leg deadlifts as the two highest-priority lower body exercises for any program, beginner through advanced.
7. Deliberately balance knee-dominant and hip-dominant/posterior-chain programming — the posterior chain is chronically undertrained relative to its injury importance.
