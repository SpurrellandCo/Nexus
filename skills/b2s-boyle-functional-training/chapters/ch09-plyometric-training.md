# Chapter 9: Plyometric Training

## Core Idea
Plyometric ("jump training") programming must progress through mastery-gated phases — starting with landing mechanics and eccentric strength, not with intensity or volume — and must balance jumps (double-leg), hops (single-leg, same-foot landing), and bounds (single-leg, opposite-foot landing) in multiple directions, because most ACL injuries occur in single-leg hop/bound scenarios that jump-only programs never train for.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Three Methods of Power Development**: A complete power-training system, of which plyometrics is only one piece.
  - When to use: Structuring the power-development portion of any program.
  - How: **Method 1 — Light-implement power** (medicine ball throws, <5 kg/~11 lb): load is light and released from the hands, giving direct access to the velocity end of the force-velocity curve. **Method 2 — Bodyweight power** (plyometrics, this chapter's subject): bodyweight is a fixed, gravity-magnified load requiring careful progression; machines like the Shuttle MVP or Total Gym Jump Trainer can reduce effective bodyweight percentage for larger/older clients. **Method 3 — Heavy-implement power** (kettlebells, Olympic bars): used by the majority of healthy clients; reserve true Olympic lifting for younger, competitive athletes — Boyle considers it a poor risk-reward choice for general older/noncompetitive adults, who should use kettlebell swings instead. Healthy athletes should be exposed to all three methods.
- **Clark's Plyometric Terminology (Jump / Hop / Bound / Skip)**: A precise, universal vocabulary to prevent dangerous misinterpretation between coaches.
  - When to use: Naming and prescribing any plyometric drill, especially when sharing programs between coaches.
  - How: **Jump** = double-leg takeoff, double-leg landing. **Hop** = single-leg takeoff, landing on the *same* foot. **Bound** = single-leg takeoff, landing on the *opposite* foot. **Skip** = single-leg takeoff with two foot contacts. Precision matters — Boyle recounts a coach who had athletes attempt his two-leg "hurdle jumps" as one-leg "hurdle hops" purely from a naming mix-up, risking real injury.
- **Gravity-Reduced vs. Gravity-Enhanced Intensity Classification**: Boyle's alternative to Don Chu's older in-place/short/long system.
  - When to use: Selecting and sequencing plyometric drill intensity across a program.
  - How: **Gravity-reduced** (lowest intensity) = jumping UP onto a box — strong concentric contraction, minimal eccentric stress because the athlete steps down afterward, negating gravity as an accelerant. **Gravity-enhanced** = jumping OVER an obstacle (hurdle) — the body must land against gravity. Progress from gravity-reduced → gravity-enhanced → semi-elastic → true elastic plyometrics (true plyometrics, minimizing ground-contact time, are reserved for Phase 4).
- **The 4-Phase Plyometric Progression** (competence-gated, not time-gated): The core program structure of the chapter.
  - When to use: Introducing or progressing any athlete's jump training, regardless of starting level (pro or middle schooler both start at Phase 1).
  - How: **Phase 1** (minimum 3 weeks, extend as needed) — jumps/hops/bounds with a stable landing; develops eccentric strength ("tendon training," the brakes on a moving vehicle); the most overlooked, most important phase — skipping or rushing it is the primary cause of plyometric injury. **Phases 2–3** — teach jumping/landing skill and introduce the elastic component; still not "true" plyometrics. **Phase 4** — true plyometrics, minimizing ground contact time and maximizing reactivity. Progress only on demonstrated competence — if an athlete can't move past Phase 1, keep them there 2–3 more weeks rather than forcing progress on a calendar.

## Key Concepts
- **Amortization phase** — the ground-contact/shock-absorption period between landing and the next takeoff; true plyometrics aim to minimize this, but that skill is only trained in Phase 4, after landing mechanics are mastered.
- **Foot contacts as the volume unit** — plyometric volume is measured by foot-ground contacts, not jump count; Boyle's system caps volume deliberately low (~25/day, ~100/week) and progresses *intensity*, not volume, across phases — the opposite of many high-foot-contact programs that risk overuse knee injury.
- **Medial vs. lateral hops** — hopping toward the body's midline (medial) is more difficult and stresses hip stabilizers differently than lateral hops; both directions are needed, not just forward/sagittal-plane work.
- **The "Idiot Box"** — Boyle's term for oversized (36–42 in / 90–105 cm) plyo boxes used for ego display; what matters is center-of-mass movement quality, not box height, so he keeps mostly 18–24 in (45–60 cm) boxes.
- **"Quiet, please"** — a landing-noise heuristic: a loud landing indicates insufficient eccentric strength for that exercise/height, and the fix is to reduce the obstacle height, not push through.

## Mental Models
- Think of Phase 1 as installing brakes before adding horsepower — eccentric strength (landing) must come before any attempt to reduce ground-contact time or add reactive intensity.
- We must crawl before we walk and walk before we run — applied literally to jump training: learn to jump and land safely before attempting to minimize time on the ground.
- Compare landing depth to takeoff depth (Jim Radcliffe's cue) as a simple, no-equipment check of whether a box is the right height — if the landing knee bend is visibly deeper than the takeoff knee bend, the box is too high.
- "Bunnies don't hop, they jump" (Mike Clark) — a memorable check for whether you're using single-leg or double-leg terminology correctly.

## Anti-patterns
- **Requiring a 2x-bodyweight back squat before starting any plyometric program**: A decades-old guideline meant only for high-level plyometrics, incorrectly generalized to all plyometric training; would disqualify ~90% of athletes and is "a ridiculous recommendation."
- **Requiring an 8-week pure-strength phase before starting plyometrics**: More rational than the 2x-bodyweight rule but still impractical — most athletes only get 10–12 weeks of off-season training, leaving too little time for a properly periodized plyometric block.
- **ACL injury-prevention programs built almost entirely on double-leg jumps** (Boyle names the Santa Monica PEP program and Sportsmetrics as examples): The actual ACL-tear mechanism is most often a single-leg hop or bound, so jump-only programming "borders on a waste of time" for the injury it's supposed to prevent.
- **Track-and-field-influenced programming that only jumps/hops forward or up**: Neglects the frontal-plane, side-to-side movement that's critical for most team sports; a functional athlete must jump, hop, and bound left and right, not just forward.
- **Using oversized "Idiot Boxes" (30–42 inches) to inflate ego**: Boyle no longer stocks 36-inch boxes at his facility — box height doesn't matter, center-of-mass control does; he calls the young men chasing box height "skin donors."
- **High foot-contact-volume programs**: A "surefire road to overuse knee injuries" — volume should stay capped (~25/day, ~100/week) while intensity, not contact count, increases across phases.
- **Skipping or rushing Phase 1 to get to "real" plyometrics**: The primary cause of plyometric-training injury, even in elite athletes, many of whom (per Boyle) are surprisingly poor at landing mechanics.

## Reference Tables
**Plyometric terminology:**

| Term | Takeoff | Landing |
|---|---|---|
| Jump | Double-leg | Double-leg |
| Hop | Single-leg | Same foot |
| Bound | Single-leg | Opposite foot |
| Skip | Single-leg | Two foot contacts |

**Phase 1 drill volume and progression (illustrative from the text):**

| Drill | Volume | Box/obstacle height |
|---|---|---|
| Box jump | 3–5 sets of 5 (up to 25 total foot contacts) | 4–24 in (10–60 cm) beginner range; avoid 30–42 in "Idiot Boxes" for general athletes |
| Single-leg box hop | 3 sets of 5 hops/leg (15/leg total) | Start as low as 4 in (10 cm); can substitute a line on the floor or 6 in mini-hurdle for anxious/younger athletes |
| Single-leg lateral box hop | 3 sets of 6/leg (3 medial + 3 lateral) | 4 in (10 cm) box or 6 in mini-hurdle/line |
| Lateral bound and stick ("Heiden"/skater hop) | Hold landing 1 full second before bounding back | Emphasize both height and distance |

**Volume/frequency guardrails:** ~25 foot contacts/day, ~100/week (even in later phases — intensity changes, not volume); NSCA originally recommended plyometrics only 2x/week (later amended to "not the same joints on consecutive days"); Boyle's system allows up to 4x/week if split into linear days and multidirectional (frontal/transverse plane) days.

**Box-height correctness check (2 criteria):** (1) Can the athlete land quietly? If not, the box is too high. (2) Is the landing knee bend the same depth as the takeoff knee bend (never deeper than a half-squat)? If landing is deeper, the box is too high.

## Worked Example
**The hurdle hop / hurdle jump miscommunication:** A California coach called Boyle to praise his athletes' ability to perform "30-inch hurdle hops" — a feat his own team couldn't replicate. Boyle realized the coach had taken his program's double-leg "hurdle jumps" and had his athletes attempt them as single-leg "hurdle hops" (per Clark's precise terminology, a hop is single-leg), based purely on Boyle's own inconsistent prior labeling. This directly illustrates why standardized plyometric terminology (jump/hop/bound/skip) isn't pedantic — a naming ambiguity nearly caused another coach's team to attempt a substantially higher-risk exercise than intended.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use all three power-development methods (light-implement/medicine ball, bodyweight/plyometric, heavy-implement/kettlebell or barbell) rather than relying on plyometrics alone.
2. Use Clark's precise terminology (jump/hop/bound/skip) to eliminate dangerous ambiguity when communicating or sharing programs.
3. Reject the 2x-bodyweight-squat and 8-week-strength-phase prerequisites for starting plyometrics — they're impractical and overly conservative; gate progression on landing competence instead.
4. Never skip or rush Phase 1 (stable-landing, eccentric-strength development) — it's the most important and most overlooked phase, and rushing it is the primary injury cause.
5. Balance jumps, hops, and bounds — and balance forward/linear work with lateral/medial-lateral work — because most real injuries (like ACL tears) occur in single-leg, multidirectional situations that jump-only, forward-only programs never rehearse.
6. Cap foot-contact volume (~25/day, ~100/week) and progress intensity instead — high-volume plyometric programming is a known driver of overuse knee injury.
7. Judge box/obstacle height by landing quietness and matched landing/takeoff depth, not by how high the athlete (or their ego) wants to jump.
