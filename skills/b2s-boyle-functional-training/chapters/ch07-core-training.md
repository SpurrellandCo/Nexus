# Chapter 7: Core Training

## Core Idea
Core training is really about motion *prevention*, not motion creation — the core musculature's primary job is isometric stabilization and resistance to extension, lateral flexion, and rotation of the trunk, so programming should train antiextension, antilateral-flexion, and antirotation, not crunches, side bends, or trunk twists; rotational power for sport comes from the hips, not the lumbar spine.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Three Core Exercise Categories (Antiextension / Antilateral Flexion / Antirotation)**: Boyle's replacement for flexion/extension/rotation-based core training, built on Sahrmann's and Porterfield & DeRosa's functional-anatomy research.
  - When to use: Selecting and sequencing every core exercise in a program.
  - How: **Antiextension** (front plank, stability ball rollout, ab wheel rollout) trains the anterior core as a stabilizer that resists extension, not as a trunk flexor — sequence this first (phases 1–3 of any program). **Antilateral flexion** (side plank and progressions) trains the quadratus lumborum and obliques as pelvis/hip stabilizers, not lateral flexors. **Antirotation** (chop and lift patterns, plank reach/row) — arguably the most important category — is built through antiextension progressions plus diagonal/rotational-resistance patterns; the program contains zero rotational exercises (no trunk twists, Russian twists, twisting sit-ups).
- **Hip Rotation, Not Lumbar Rotation, for Power**: The mechanical basis for medicine ball training and for rejecting "core rotation" exercises.
  - When to use: Designing any power-development work for striking/throwing sports (baseball, golf, hockey, tennis).
  - How: Recognize that the golf swing, baseball swing, and similar striking skills are accomplished through hip internal/external rotation with a relatively stable lumbar spine, not through lumbar rotation. Train rotary power via hip-driven medicine ball throws against a wall, never via loaded lumbar-rotation exercises.
- **Breathing as Core Training (Postural Restoration Institute model, via Michael Mullin)**: Reframes the "draw-in" cue as a function of proper exhalation mechanics.
  - When to use: Coaching any core exercise, especially isometric holds.
  - How: On inhalation, the diaphragm contracts concentrically and flattens (like a parachute being pulled down); late in a maximal exhalation, the deep abdominals contract concentrically, pushing the diaphragm back into its dome shape. The old "draw-in" cue is really the concentric contraction portion of a complete exhalation. Coach lengthened inhale/exhale cycles (the "blowing up a balloon without removing it from your mouth" drill) rather than counting time or breaths.
- **Time-Under-Tension Core Progression**: A stabilization-specific alternative to standard rep progressions.
  - When to use: Progressing isometric core holds (planks, bridges) week to week.
  - How: Stabilization exercises start at 3 sets of 25 seconds, broken into five 5-second holds (per physical therapist Al Visnick: "if you want to train the stabilizers, you have to give them time to stabilize" — a 1-second hold is insufficient). For bodyweight rep-based exercises, use the standard 3×8 (week 1) → 3×10 (week 2) → 3×12 (week 3) progression, then advance to the next numbered progression and reduce reps.

## Key Concepts
- **Core (broad definition)** — all muscles of the body's midsection: rectus abdominis, transversus abdominis, multifidus, internal/external obliques, quadratus lumborum, spinal erectors, and (to some extent) glutes, hamstrings, and hip rotators.
- **Antirotation** — the core's key function: resisting rotation of the trunk relative to the pelvis, rather than producing it; per Sahrmann, total lumbar rotational range of motion is only ~13°, with the greatest single-segment rotation (5°) between L5-S1 — far too little to be a useful "power" source, and risky to load.
- **The thoracic spine as the intended site of trunk rotation** — per Sahrmann, rotational exercises should be coached to feel like the motion is happening "in the area of the chest," not the low back.
- **Bilateral limb deficit is not discussed here** — but the same "isolation for innervation" logic (Ch 1) applies: core exercises "activate or upregulate" muscles more than they fatigue them, described as a dimmer-switch effect, not a maximal-strength stimulus.
- **Special vs. specific strength distinction, applied to medicine ball training** — medicine ball work is "plyometrics for the core stabilizers and hip rotators," converting the strength/stability built by planks and bridges into usable, ground-to-hand summation of force.

## Mental Models
- Core muscles are stabilizers, not movers — ask "how many sports involve trunk flexion/extension as a skill?" (very few) the same way Ch 1 asks "how many sports are played sitting down?"
- Think of core activation cues as a dimmer switch, not an on/off switch — you're turning up the power to muscles that are already working, not fatiguing fresh muscle.
- "A strong core has nothing to do with low body fat" — abdominal visibility is a function of diet, not core training; don't let athletes conflate the two goals.
- If it looks too heavy for a medicine ball throw, it is — emphasize throw velocity over ball weight; struggle to throw = wrong ball.

## Anti-patterns
- **Crunches, sit-ups, side bends, Russian twists, and trunk-twist exercises as core training**: Train the core as a mover (flexor/rotator) instead of as the stabilizer it actually is; potentially reinforces the lumbar-spine dysfunction pattern the chapter is trying to eliminate.
- **Stretches or dynamic drills that increase lumbar rotational range of motion** (seated/lying trunk rotational stretches, dynamic bent-leg or straight-leg trunk twists, the scorpion): Explicitly eliminated from Boyle's programs — per Sahrmann, increasing lumbar ROM is "more dangerous than beneficial," and Boyle reports a real decrease in low-back-pain complaints after removing these.
- **Loaded lumbar-rotation exercises for "core power" (e.g., loaded trunk twists)**: A "potentially dangerous mistake" — rotary power for striking/throwing lives in the hips, not the lumbar spine.
- **Judging core work by the burn**: Listed as a genuine disadvantage of medicine ball training — athletes often don't feel medicine ball work until the next day, unlike crunch-style burn-based core work, and coaches must not mistake absence of burn for absence of effect.
- **Ignoring breathing mechanics during core coaching**: Treating the "draw-in" as a static cue rather than coaching it as the concentric contraction phase of a full exhalation misses the actual muscular mechanism.

**Medicine ball training — advantages vs. disadvantages:**

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Works in a sports-general position/pattern (golf/tennis/baseball swing-like) | You don't feel it — effects show up the next day, not as a "burn" |
| Bridges conventional core strength/endurance work to power development ("plyometrics for the core") | Requires significant space and a masonry wall |
| Teaches summation of force: ground → legs → core → arms | Requires many medicine balls across a range of sizes |
| Can be done solo against a wall | — |
| Total-body conditioning effect | — |

**Medicine ball selection rule of thumb:** if in doubt about weight, go down 2 lb (1 kg); for baseball overhead throws, stay at 4 lb (2 kg); nonbouncing balls preferred for most throws (8 lb/4 kg standard, 6 lb/3 kg mini-ball for kids) — they eliminate the unwanted eccentric "catch and decelerate" component that a bouncing ball forces, which better matches striking's 1RM, non-repeated-effort nature; overhead throws remain an exception, still done with light rubber (bouncing) balls caught after one bounce.

## Worked Example
**Why nonbouncing medicine balls changed Boyle's rotational-throw programming:**
Boyle initially favored bouncing rubber medicine balls for rotational side-throws because the ball's rebound off the wall created a rhythmic, plyometric "catch and re-accelerate" pattern. After acquiring lighter nonbouncing balls for younger athletes, he tried a side-twist throw with one and found it barely rebounded. Rather than discard the tool, he asked: is the eccentric/deceleration component from the ball bouncing back actually important for the skill being trained? He concluded no — striking skills (a golf swing, a slap shot) are essentially single, powerful, non-repeated efforts, not a rhythmic bounce-and-recatch pattern. He switched most rotational throws (side throws, slams) to nonbouncing balls, keeping bouncing rubber balls only for overhead throws. This is a direct example of Boyle re-deriving an exercise choice from the actual demands of the skill rather than from training tradition.

## Key Takeaways
1. Train the core to prevent motion (antiextension, antilateral flexion, antirotation), not to create it — abandon crunches, side bends, and trunk twists.
2. Rotary power for striking/throwing sports comes from hip internal/external rotation with a stable lumbar spine, not from lumbar rotation — never load lumbar rotation.
3. Eliminate stretches and dynamic drills that seek to increase lumbar rotational range of motion; total lumbar rotation is only ~13° and increasing it is more dangerous than beneficial.
4. Coach breathing as an active part of core training: inhale through the nose (diaphragm flattens), exhale fully through the mouth (deep abdominals contract) — the "draw-in" is the tail end of a complete exhalation.
5. Progress isometric core holds by time-under-tension (5-second holds, building to 3×25 sec total) rather than treating them like standard strength sets.
6. Use medicine ball throws against a wall (not partners) to convert core/hip stability into usable power; prioritize throw velocity over ball weight, and match ball type (bouncing vs. nonbouncing) to whether the skill involves a rhythmic catch-recatch or a single powerful effort.
7. Place core work throughout the session as an "active rest" component rather than confining it strictly to the start or end.
