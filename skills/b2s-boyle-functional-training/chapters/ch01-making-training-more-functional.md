# Chapter 1: Making Training More Functional

## Core Idea
Functional training means purposeful, sports-general training done standing, with the feet in contact with the ground, using multijoint movement — not "sport-specific" mimicry of a sport's skills in the weight room.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Three Questions Test**: A diagnostic Boyle uses to define what functional training is.
  - When to use: Evaluating whether an exercise or program design belongs in a functional program.
  - How: Ask (1) How many sports are played sitting down? (2) How many sports are played in a rigid environment where stability is provided by outside sources? (3) How many sports skills are performed by one joint acting in isolation? Answer to all three is "almost none" — so functional exercises should be done standing, without externally-stabilized machines, and should be multijoint.
- **Sports-General Training** (vs. sport-specific training): The premise that most sports share far more movement commonalities (sprinting, striking, jumping, moving laterally) than differences, so a strength program should train general qualities (speed, core, push/pull/hip patterns) rather than imitate the sport's skills.
  - When to use: Designing a program for any sport — Boyle's own example is training an Olympic judo gold medalist and an Olympic ice hockey gold medalist with remarkably similar programs.
  - How: Build around common movement patterns (push, pull, knee-dominant, hip-dominant, core) rather than sport-mimicking drills.
- **Functional Anatomy (Gary Gray's kinetic-chain model)**: Replaces "origin-insertion" (isolated-joint) anatomy with a model where, during ground contact, all lower-body muscles act together — first eccentrically to decelerate flexion at ankle/knee/hip, then concentrically to drive extension.
  - When to use: Justifying why closed-chain, standing, ground-based exercises train muscles the way they're actually used in sport, while open-chain/single-joint exercises (e.g., leg extension) do not.
  - How: Recognize that during landing, the quadriceps is not acting as a "knee extensor" — it's contracting eccentrically to prevent knee flexion; hamstrings are simultaneously preventing knee and hip flexion. Muscles that appear antagonists (quads/hamstrings) are actually agonists working together.
- **The Functional Paradox / "Isolation for Innervation"** (credited to Mark Verstegen): Some single-joint, apparently "nonfunctional" exercises are necessary to properly activate key stabilizer muscle groups before they can contribute to functional, multijoint movement.
  - When to use: Deciding whether to include isolated exercises for the deep abdominals, hip abductors/rotators, or scapular stabilizers.
  - How: Use brief, small-range-of-motion isolation work to "turn on" these three stabilizer groups, then let them function within larger multijoint movements.

## Key Concepts
- **Function** — purpose; in training, "purposeful" training that transfers to how the body is actually used in sport.
- **Open-chain** — the foot (or working limb) is not in contact with the ground/a stable platform; considered near-synonymous with "nonfunctional" for lower-extremity training.
- **Closed-chain** — the foot is in contact with the ground, allowing muscles to work together as they do during real movement.
- **Sport-specific training** — practicing the actual skills of a sport on the field/court/mat (Boyle reserves this term for skill practice, not strength training).
- **Cross-training** — mistakenly using one sport's training methods (e.g., powerlifting, Olympic lifting) as the primary training model for a different sport; Boyle distinguishes this from true functional training.
- **Proprioception** — internal sensory feedback about body position and movement; degraded by machine-based training that provides external stability.
- **Scapular stabilizers** — one of three key muscle groups (with deep abdominals and hip abductors/rotators) that Boyle says require dedicated stability training, not just "functional" multijoint work.

## Mental Models
- Think of the stability continuum as running from Gary Gray's multiplanar, unstable, ground-based approach on one end to the Westside Barbell powerlifting approach on the other — most programs should sit toward the functional end but borrow strength methodology as needed.
- Use single-leg (unilateral) exercises whenever you want to intentionally introduce controlled instability, because "asking an athlete to stand on one leg" is the simplest way to force the body to re-stabilize itself.
- Train movements, not muscles: balance push vs. pull strength, and knee-dominant hip extension (quads/glutes) vs. hip-dominant hip extension (hamstrings/glutes), rather than chasing size or strength in one muscle group.

## Anti-patterns
- **Machine-based, seated, single-joint training as a primary strength method**: Fails all three of the diagnostic questions — seated, externally stabilized, and single-joint — and, despite being safer during training, likely increases injury risk during competition because it doesn't develop proprioception or self-stabilization.
- **"This happens in sports all the time" as a justification for risky exercise positions**: E.g., loading a squat with a flexed spine because a baseball player fields a ground ball that way. Boyle's rule: never compromise back safety to make an exercise position more sport-specific.
- **Confusing functional training with balance-ball/stability-ball gimmicks**: An early misperception of the field that gave critics an easy — and inaccurate — target.
- **Over-applying "functional" purity and neglecting stabilizers**: Chasing multijoint, standing exercises exclusively while neglecting the deep abdominals, hip abductors/rotators, and scapular stabilizers, which often need isolated "innervation" work first.

## Worked Example
**Kayla Harrison (Olympic judo gold medalist) — a sports-general training day:**
Boyle's team did not try to imitate judo throws. Program design was driven by injury history and the sport's general demands (high practice volume, high-impact body stress, year-round schedule), not by mimicking judo skills. A typical training day:
1. Foam roller
2. Stretching
3. Dynamic warm-up
4. Power: medicine ball drills and plyometrics
5. Strength: push (dumbbell bench press), pull (ring row), knee-dominant (single-leg squat), hip-dominant (single-leg straight-leg deadlift)
6. Core: done between sets — plank, side plank, carries
7. Conditioning: sport-matched four-minute energy-system bouts, done primarily on a stationary bike to reduce joint stress

This is the same push/pull/knee-dominant/hip-dominant/core template Boyle used for hockey athletes — the program stays similar across very different sports, which is the point of sports-general training.

## Key Takeaways
1. Apply the three-questions test to any candidate exercise: standing, self-stabilized, multijoint wins by default.
2. Most sports share far more movement commonality than difference — build programs around general qualities (push, pull, knee-dominant, hip-dominant, core, speed), not sport mimicry.
3. During ground contact, lower-body muscles act as a coordinated unit (eccentric deceleration → concentric extension) — this is the anatomical basis for preferring closed-chain, standing exercise.
4. Not all isolation work is bad — the deep abdominals, hip abductors/rotators, and scapular stabilizers often need isolated "innervation" before they'll function properly in compound movement.
5. Never sacrifice spinal safety to make an exercise look more "sport realistic."
6. The primary measure of a strength and conditioning program's success is player health/injury reduction, not just performance.
