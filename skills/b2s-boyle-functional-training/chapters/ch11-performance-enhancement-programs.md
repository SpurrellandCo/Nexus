# Chapter 11: Performance Enhancement Programs

## Core Idea
Sport-specific programming is a myth for strength training — the same 9 components and phase structure apply across nearly all sports, because strength is strength — but conditioning must be genuinely sport-specific, built around acceleration, deceleration, and change of direction rather than sport-mimicking distance or arbitrary fitness tests.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **"Strength Is Strength" (Program Universality)**: The chapter's central claim, extending Ch 2's sports-general philosophy to program design itself.
  - When to use: Deciding whether to build a separate strength program per sport.
  - How: Ask "is a fast baseball player different from a fast football or soccer player?" — no. The only sport-specific adjustment to strength programming is for overhead athletes (baseball, tennis, swimming, volleyball), who get reduced overhead-pressing volume to protect the shoulder. What actually varies by sport is *how many days per week* are available for training, not the methods used.
- **The 9 Essential Program Components**: The complete menu from which every strength session is built, regardless of training-day count.
  - When to use: Constructing any strength session template.
  - How: (1) Explosive power development (Olympic lifts, plyometrics, swings, jump squats); (2) Bilateral hip-dominant exercises (trap-bar deadlift, kettlebell sumo deadlift, goblet squat); (3) Single-leg knee-dominant exercises (single-leg squats, split squats); (4) Unilateral hip-dominant exercises (straight-leg deadlifts); (5) Core work (antiextension, antirotation, antilateral flexion); (6) Horizontal presses (bench, incline); (7) Vertical presses (dumbbell/kettlebell overhead); (8) Horizontal pulls (rows); (9) Vertical pulls (chin-ups). More training days = some components trained twice weekly; a 2-day program addresses each component only once, forcing the simplest form: an explosive exercise, a push, a knee-dominant exercise, a pull, and a hip-dominant exercise.
- **The Tri-Set / Density Model**: Boyle's session-structuring method for maximizing "work per hour."
  - When to use: Sequencing exercises within a single training session.
  - How: Explosive/power work goes first ("the fast stuff gets done first"). Rest intervals between sets of the primary strength pair are filled with core or mobility work, not passed idle — this pairing is called a tri-set. Remaining accessory/auxiliary exercises run as another tri-set or a mini-circuit (2–3 sets/circuits, ≤1 minute rest between). Programming is fundamentally about density — how much quality work fits in the available hour.
- **The Poliquin Undulating 4-Phase Model (3 weeks each, 12-week off-season)**: The macro-periodization structure for a full off-season.
  - When to use: Structuring an entire off-season strength block.
  - How: **Phase 1 (accumulation/base/anatomical adaptation)** — 2×8-10 in week 1, progressing to 3 sets in weeks 2–3; Olympic lifts at 5 reps. **Phase 2 (intensification)** — volume drops (24 reps → 9–15 reps total), intensity rises (70% → low 80% range); e.g., sets of 3 for bench/chin-ups/Olympic lifts, sets of 5 for lower-body strength work. **Phase 3 (second accumulation, varied methods)** — complex training (strength exercise paired with a power exercise), eccentric emphasis, or a modified 10-5-20 three-set scheme; total volume climbs back toward 24 reps. **Phase 4 (strength-endurance, sport-dependent)** — higher reps to prepare for preseason practice for most sports, or another lower-rep strength phase for high-absolute-strength sports like football.
- **Charlie Francis's High-Low Conditioning Model**: A simple intensity-distribution rule to prevent conditioning overreach.
  - When to use: Structuring the weekly mix of hard vs. easy conditioning sessions.
  - How: Every conditioning session should be either above 90% effort or under 80% effort — nothing in between. Boyle's application: one hard conditioning day per week, two or three days at ~70% effort (tempo runs, slide-board work, or biking).

## Key Concepts
- **Training plan as recipe, not menu** (extends the cook/chef analogy from Ch 4) — you can't add or remove program items without affecting the outcome; the 6-step session recipe is: (1) foam rolling, (2) static stretching, (3) mobility/activation/dynamic warm-up, (4) power work (medicine ball, plyometrics, speed), (5) power and strength work in the weight room, (6) conditioning.
- **Extensive tempo running → shuttle running progression** — the sport-general conditioning base-building sequence (from Ch 5), starting with striding (30–100 yd) interspersed with walking recovery (30–40 yd), 6–8 minutes initially, once/week, then progressing to change-of-direction shuttle runs (once/week) on a 25- or 50-yard course, initially reducing total distance (~1,000 yd tempo → ~750 yd shuttle) to offset the higher muscular stress of direction changes, then increasing 10–20%/week.
- **Negative rest-to-work ratio** — a conditioning demand unique to wrestling/combat sports, where rest between periods is shorter than the period itself; these athletes get most of their conditioning from practice itself and should use additional conditioning judiciously (especially around weight-cutting).
- **Minimal effective dose** — the guiding conditioning-program-design principle: use bodyweight when practical, target ~16–20 sets per one-hour workout, and never simulate a strength sport (bodybuilding/powerlifting/Olympic lifting) when the actual goal is preparing an athlete to play their sport.

## Mental Models
- "Tell me how much time you have and I'll tell you which program to use" — training-day count (2/3/4-day), not sport, is the primary variable that should determine program structure.
- Think outside the box for conditioning tools — slide boards and weighted vests are Boyle's two "not-so-obvious" tools that make conditioning genuinely sport- and movement-specific where running alone cannot.
- Consider whether you're "adding stress to an already-stressed system" — for year-round athletes (basketball, soccer, hockey), off-season conditioning should complement, not duplicate, the sport's own on-court/on-ice stress.
- Ask multisport or year-round high schoolers "what else are you planning to do today?" — accounts for hidden training load from pickup games, extra practice, etc. before adding more.

## Anti-patterns
- **Designing separate strength programs per sport based on the assumption that "our sport is different"**: The chapter's central rejection — nearly all team and many individual sports share the same underlying speed/power/strength needs; testing may differ by sport, but training should not.
- **Conditioning programs that ignore change of direction entirely (straight-line running or cycling only)**: Fails to address the actual mechanism of most game injuries (acceleration/deceleration), leaving athletes "not in game shape" despite passing prescribed conditioning to the letter.
- **Chasing an "aerobic base" through steady-state jogging/cycling as conditioning prep**: Produces negative physiological/tissue changes and overuse injury risk for sprint-dominant-sport athletes; replace with the tempo-run → shuttle-run progression instead.
- **Pitchers using distance running to prepare for "100+ pitch" endurance**: A mismatch of energy systems — actual games show ~10-12 pitches/inning with ~15 minutes rest, so interval training, not distance running, matches the real demand.
- **Ignoring sport equipment weight in conditioning (football, hockey)**: "Foolish" per Boyle — these are the two sports where equipment adds meaningful load; his athletes train the back half of summer conditioning wearing 10 lb (5 kg) weight belts on the slide board specifically to acclimate to game-day equipment weight.
- **Running timed change-of-direction shuttles on every single conditioning day**: The backlash Boyle references against overworking athletes — apply the high-low model (one hard day, several ~70% days) instead of maximal-effort work every session.
- **Keeping hockey players on flexion-posture spin bikes**: Reinforces the "skater's crouch" posture problem rather than correcting it; Boyle prefers Airdyne/Assault AirBikes for their upright posture and added upper-body component.

## Reference Tables
**Training-day program structure by frequency:**

| Frequency | Use case | Structure notes |
|---|---|---|
| 4-day | Preferred for off-season, most sports | Easiest to fit all 9 components; room for extra core/prehab work; some components trained twice/week |
| 3-day | Minimum recommended off-season volume for most team sports | 25% less training time than 4-day; still balances key components with some compromise; every day starts with an explosive exercise + primary pair + tri-set |
| 2-day | In-season only | Most difficult to design; each of the 9 components addressed only once; simplified to explosive + push + knee-dominant + pull + hip-dominant; typically opens with hang clean/snatch/dumbbell snatch; incline press used as a push/overhead compromise |

**4-phase periodization volume/intensity summary:**

| Phase | Type | Volume | Intensity |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Accumulation/base | 2×8-10 → 3×8-10 (weeks 1→2-3); Olympic lifts ×5 | Lower |
| 2 | Intensification | 24 reps → 9-15 reps total (e.g., 3×3 upper/Olympic, 3×5 lower) | 70% → low 80% range |
| 3 | Second accumulation (varied method) | Back toward 24 reps total | Complex training, eccentric emphasis, or 10-5-20 scheme |
| 4 | Strength-endurance (or lower-rep strength for high-absolute-strength sports) | Higher reps (most sports) | Prepares for preseason practice |

**Sport-specific conditioning notes:**

| Sport | Key guidance |
|---|---|
| Football | Off-season interval running required (not biking/circuits); NFL injury increases linked to reduced off-season running/OTAs |
| Baseball | On-demand speed, not high aerobic fitness; interval/sprint training over distance running (even for pitchers); 3 distinct player groups (position players, starters, relievers) have different conditioning needs |
| Basketball | Year-round court play; use bike + slide-board to add lateral conditioning while reducing joint pounding |
| Ice hockey | Near year-round now; run in off-season to counter "skater's crouch"; avoid flexion-posture spin bikes, prefer Airdyne/Assault bikes; don't run and skate same day |
| Soccer | Aerobic-focused training is counterproductive, especially for developing players; use tempo/shuttle running, not jogging — "the training does not need to look like the testing" |
| Wrestling/combat sports | Negative rest-to-work ratio; most conditioning comes from practice itself; be judicious with extra conditioning around weight-cutting |
| Multisport high schoolers | Need a true off-season if playing ≤2 sports; year-round single-sport athletes need deliberate rest/work balance |

**Slide board — unique conditioning capabilities (per the chapter, "most bang for the buck"):** places the athlete in a sport-specific bent-knee position (nearly all sports); positively stresses abductors/adductors for injury prevention; supports 3–4 athletes on one piece of equipment; under $1,000 for group interval conditioning; recommended 2 of 4 training days/week for all sports except rowing.

## Worked Example
**The tempo-run-to-shuttle-run conditioning base progression (concrete numbers from the text):** Athletes begin with 6–8 minutes of tempo running once per week — striding 30–100 yards depending on facility size, walking 30–40 yards recovery after each stride, accumulating roughly 1,000 total yards, preceded by up to 20 minutes of dynamic warm-up (yielding ~30 minutes of elevated heart rate overall). They then progress to shuttle runs (also once/week) on a 25- or 50-yard course, initially at a reduced total volume (~750 yards) to offset the added muscular stress of direction changes, then increasing volume 10–20%/week (~150 yd increments). This is Boyle's concrete answer to "how do you build a conditioning base without jogging" — work backward from short, high-quality tempo work toward change-of-direction-specific shuttle work, never toward long steady-state jogging.

## Key Takeaways
1. Strength programming is not sport-specific (with the single exception of reduced overhead pressing for overhead-sport athletes) — the same 9 components and phase structure apply broadly.
2. Available training days per week, not sport, is what should determine program structure (2/3/4-day templates).
3. Sequence sessions as explosive work first, then strength pairs (tri-sets with core/mobility filling rest), then accessory circuits — programming is about density (work per hour).
4. Use the 4-phase undulating model (accumulation → intensification → accumulation → strength-endurance/strength) across a 12-week off-season.
5. Conditioning, unlike strength, must be genuinely sport-specific — built around each sport's actual work:rest ratio, motor pattern, and directional demands, not a generic aerobic-base assumption.
6. Progress conditioning bases through tempo running → shuttle running, never long steady-state jogging, for sprint-dominant sport athletes.
7. Apply Charlie Francis's high-low rule (>90% or <80% effort, nothing in between) to prevent conditioning burnout — one hard day, several easy days per week.
8. Use the slide board and weighted vests/belts as the two most sport-specific, most overlooked conditioning tools, especially for hockey and football.
