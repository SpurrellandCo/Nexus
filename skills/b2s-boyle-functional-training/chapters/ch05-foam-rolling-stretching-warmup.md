# Chapter 5: Foam Rolling, Stretching, and Dynamic Warm-Up

## Core Idea
Warm-up should be sequenced as foam rolling → static stretching → dynamic warm-up, because each addresses a different tissue/joint problem (density, length, and motor readiness respectively), and mobility/stability training should be targeted per joint using the joint-by-joint approach rather than applied uniformly to the whole body.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Joint-by-Joint Approach** (developed with physical therapist Gray Cook): The body is a stack of joints that alternate between needing mobility and needing stability; dysfunction at one joint shows up as pain at the joint above or below.
  - When to use: Deciding what kind of work (mobility/rolling/stretching vs. strength/stability training) a given joint needs, and diagnosing the true source of pain at another joint.
  - How: Ankle → mobility. Knee → stability. Hip → mobility (but can also be unstable — needs both). Lumbar spine → stability (avoid rotary ROM work here). Thoracic spine → mobility. Glenohumeral (shoulder) → mobility, trained for stability. Apply the rule "lose ankle mobility, get knee pain; lose hip mobility, get low back pain; lose thoracic mobility, get neck/shoulder or low back pain" — treat the joint below/above the pain site, not just the painful joint itself.
- **The Three-Step Warm-Up Sequence**: Boyle reversed his own earlier position (1st edition said never stretch before exercise) after learning that timing, not stretching itself, was the problem.
  - When to use: Every training session's warm-up.
  - How: (1) Foam roll 5–10 minutes to decrease tissue density/knots first ("ironing for the muscles"); (2) Static stretch while tissue is most receptive to length change — some experts now recommend stretching cold, before any warm-up; (3) Dynamic warm-up last, to negate any power-output decrease from static stretching and prepare the nervous system for activity. Postworkout static stretching does not produce flexibility gains — it must happen near the start of the session.
- **Developing Linear Speed Safely (3-phase, 9-week acceleration program)**: A team-sport-appropriate speed program built around acceleration, not top-end speed.
  - When to use: Any team-sport athlete's linear speed development, especially in-season or with large groups where injury risk from all-out competitive sprinting must be managed.
  - How: **Weeks 1–3 (noncompetitive speed)** — 3–5 hard pushes then coast, slightly below full speed, no racing; primary drills: lean-fall-and-run, 90° lean-fall-and-run; ~six 10-yard sprints/day. **Weeks 4–6 (short competitive speed)** — ball-drop sprints from various starts, capping most efforts under ~7 yards, introduces competitive intensity without excessive hamstring/hip-flexor stress. **Weeks 7–9 (long competitive speed)** — partner chase sprints and breakaway-belt sprints/tag games in a 10–20 yard zone; maximum competitive effort. Rule: increase volume OR intensity each phase, never both at once.
- **Special Strength vs. Specific Strength (sled training)**: A distinction for classifying resisted-speed tools.
  - When to use: Choosing and sequencing sled work across a training cycle.
  - How: **Special strength** = resistance movements that incorporate the joint dynamics of the skill without imitating it exactly (e.g., heavy sled marching — Boyle considers this possibly the single best speed development tool). **Specific strength** = resistance movements that directly imitate the joint action (e.g., sled running/sprinting with lighter load). MBSC sequence: 6 weeks heavy sled marching, then 6 weeks lighter sled sprints, within a 12-week cycle. Reject the traditional "10% bodyweight / 10% velocity decrement" rule — load should be governed by whether the athlete can hold correct acceleration posture/mechanics, not an arbitrary percentage; adjust load for surface (lighter on grass, heavier on turf) and sled type (flat sled = more friction than double-runner).

## Key Concepts
- **Creep** — the property of soft tissue to elongate and stiffen under sustained low load (e.g., 20 minutes of slouched sitting increases posterior spinal ligament laxity per McGill & Brown 1992); the physiological rationale for foam rolling.
- **"Locked long" and "locked short"** (Thomas Myers) — tissue states resulting from creep; posterior-chain tissue (upper/lower back, glutes, hamstrings) tends to become "locked long" from sitting, which is why it's rolled but not necessarily stretched, while anterior tissue is rolled AND stretched.
- **Self-myofascial release** (Mike Clark) — the technical term for foam rolling as self-massage; originally conceived as self-acupressure on trigger points/knots.
- **Mobility vs. flexibility** — flexibility targets muscles and requires static holds; mobility targets joints and requires gentle motion (Shirley Sahrmann: "get the right muscle moving the right joint at the right time"). Mobility work should target only the joints that need it (per the joint-by-joint map).
- **Acceleration vs. speed** — sport is about acceleration (0–10 yards), not top-end speed (not reached until ~60m even in track); the 10/20/40-yard dash are acceleration tests, not true speed tests. Track-derived "pulling/pawing" mechanics have limited application to sport's push-dominant, forward-leaning acceleration mechanics.
- **The Weyland study** (2000, *Journal of Applied Physiology*) — "faster top running speeds are achieved with greater ground forces, not more rapid leg movements," supporting force-production training (sleds, strength) over pure technique drilling.

## Mental Models
- Think of foam rolling as "ironing for the muscles" — a necessary precursor to stretching, not a substitute for it.
- "Pain never precedes dysfunction" (Stanley Paris) — treat dysfunction (poor joint mobility/stability) proactively; don't wait for pain to identify the problem, and when pain does appear, look one joint up or down the chain rather than only at the painful site.
- "If it hurts, don't do it" is the opposite rule for foam rolling — deliberately seek and work tender/trigger-point areas ("hurts so good"), but distinguish moderate discomfort from an injurious sensation, and it should never cause bruising.
- Icing a sore knee without examining the ankle or hip is like pulling the battery out of a smoke detector to silence it (Hyman & Liponis) — treat the alarm as information about a problem elsewhere in the chain.

## Anti-patterns
- **Static stretching immediately before high-intensity activity with no dynamic warm-up after**: 1980s research showing static stretching decreases power output led to blanket avoidance; the fix is sequencing (stretch early, dynamic warm-up right before activity), not elimination.
- **Postworkout-only static stretching as the flexibility strategy**: Doesn't produce flexibility gains; stretching must happen near the start of the session, before dynamic warm-up.
- **Chasing lumbar spine rotational range of motion**: Sahrmann and Porterfield/DeRosa both indicate lumbar rotation ROM work is more dangerous than beneficial; the lumbar spine needs stability, not mobility work — target thoracic spine mobility instead when more "core" rotation feels needed.
- **Following the traditional "10% rule" for sled loading**: An arbitrary percentage-based load/velocity-decrement cap that ignores motor-pattern quality; Boyle argues load should be governed by whether the athlete maintains correct acceleration mechanics, and heavy sled marching in particular can exceed bodyweight loads safely.
- **Treating flexibility and mobility as interchangeable**: They target different tissues (muscle vs. joint) and require different interventions (static hold vs. gentle motion) — conflating them misdirects programming.

## Reference Tables
**Foam rolling target areas and technique notes:**

| Area | Technique note |
|---|---|
| Gluteus maximus & hip rotators | Sit on roller, slight tilt toward side; cross leg to place hip rotators on stretch |
| Low back (spinal erectors, quadratus lumborum) | Tilt right/left; skip if any spinal injury concern |
| Upper back / thoracic spine | Touch elbows together to protract scapulae and expose thoracic vertebrae to the roller |
| TFL & gluteus medius | Prone, roller under TFL below iliac crest; then rotate 90° to side position for glute medius |
| Adductors | Floor technique (beginners) or table/box-drop technique (advanced) — work in thirds along the femur, from above the knee to near the pubic symphysis |
| Posterior shoulder | Side-lying, arm draped over roller, roll face-down to face-up to hit lat and rotator cuff |
| Pectorals | Prone, roller parallel to body, arm draped over; males roll side-to-side, females reach overhead |

General protocol: 5–10 minutes total, ~10 slow rolls per position or roll until pain dissipates, done daily, before the workout.

## Worked Example
**The 2000 speed-development field test:** Boyle's 3-phase acceleration system was field-tested with 400 athletes across ~19,000 workouts (4 days/week × 12 weeks) in summer 2000, yielding fewer than 10 groin/hamstring strains total. The protocol requires ≥15 minutes of dynamic warm-up and agility work before every speed session, with plyometrics done after warm-up but before sprinting (as a "speed-of-contraction bridge"). Combined with progressive lower-body strength (knee-extension and hip-extension patterns, twice weekly) and progressive plyometrics, this produced an injury rate of less than 1 per 1,000 workouts at MBSC — Boyle's core evidence that a conservative, phased acceleration progression is both effective and safe for team-sport groups.

## Key Takeaways
1. Sequence warm-up as foam roll → static stretch → dynamic warm-up; don't skip static stretching, just move it earlier and always follow it with dynamic work.
2. Use the joint-by-joint map to target mobility work only at joints that need mobility (ankle, hip, thoracic spine, glenohumeral) and stability/strength work at joints that need stability (knee, lumbar spine, scapula).
3. When pain shows up at a joint, check the joint immediately above and below it before assuming the painful joint itself is dysfunctional.
4. Train acceleration (0–10 yards), not top-end speed, for team-sport athletes — the 10/20/40-yard dash are acceleration tests.
5. Progress speed development through 3 phases over 9 weeks: noncompetitive → short competitive → long competitive, increasing volume or intensity (never both) each phase.
6. Sled work splits into special strength (heavy marching) and specific strength (lighter sprinting); load should be dictated by maintained acceleration mechanics, not an arbitrary 10% rule.
7. Avoid lumbar rotational mobility work — the lumbar spine needs stability; direct "core rotation" desires toward thoracic mobility instead.
