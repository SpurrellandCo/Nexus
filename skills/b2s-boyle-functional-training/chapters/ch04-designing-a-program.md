# Chapter 4: Designing a Program

## Core Idea
Program design should follow a disciplined, mostly-copied "recipe" appropriate to the designer's experience level (cook → sous-chef → chef), built on progressive resistance, mastery-before-loading progressions, and simple periodization — not an ad hoc mix of borrowed ideas from multiple sources.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Cook / Sous-Chef / Chef Proficiency Model**: A self-assessment for how much a coach should deviate from an established program template.
  - When to use: Before designing or modifying any program — first honestly place yourself on the continuum.
  - How: **Cook** (beginner, first program) — find a good "recipe" (proven program) and follow it exactly, don't mix sources. **Sous-chef** (3rd–4th year coach) — can alter ingredients without spoiling the plan, understands ratios matter. **Chef** (5+ years successful program design) — can make bold, informed changes. Never mix bits of multiple programs as a beginner; that produces a poor "combination of recipes."
- **Essentials of Program Design** (4 principles): The core ruleset for building any functional strength program.
  - When to use: Every time a new exercise, progression, or program block is introduced.
  - How: (1) Learn basic patterns first — master bodyweight technique before loading; (2) Begin with simple bodyweight exercises — if an athlete fails with external load but succeeds at bodyweight, remove the load, don't push through; (3) Progress from simple to complex following established progressions (e.g., split squat → rear-foot-elevated split squat); (4) Use progressive resistance — add weight or reps nearly every week (credited historically to Milo of Crete and his growing bull).
- **Baseline / Progression / Regression Exercise Classification**: Boyle's system for categorizing every upper body, lower body, and core exercise.
  - When to use: Selecting and advancing exercises within a program.
  - How: **Baseline** = general starting point for the average athlete, held for 3 weeks by default. **Progression** = numbered steps forward (Progression 3 = quite difficult) via added load or altered bodyweight leverage. **Regression** = numbered steps backward (Regression 3 = very simple) used immediately when an athlete struggles with the baseline due to injury or technical issues. Mastery — judged by the coach's "eye test" — gates every advance.
- **The Functional Continuum**: A taxonomy for rating any exercise from least to most functional.
  - When to use: Evaluating whether to include or replace a given exercise in a program.
  - How: For knee-dominant lower-body work, the least-to-most-functional order is: (1) lying leg press (supine, machine-stabilized) → (2) standing machine squat (standing, still machine-stabilized, bilateral) → (3) barbell squat (standing, self-stabilized, bilateral) → (4) single-leg squat (standing, self-stabilized, unilateral — engages the trunk and lower body the way running/jumping does). Apply the same logic (stability source → stance → joint count) to upper body push/pull and core exercises.
- **Basic Periodization (Poliquin/Dan John model)**: A deliberately simple accumulation/intensification/unloading cycle.
  - When to use: Structuring set/rep schemes across a training block.
  - How: Alternate higher-volume, lower-load phases (e.g., 3×8 = 24 reps) with higher-intensity, lower-volume phases (e.g., 3×5 = 15 reps), per Charles Poliquin's 1988 framing; Dan John's rule of thumb is 15–25 total reps for major exercises per session. Boyle's own default cycle: sets of 8–10 (accumulation) → sets of 3 (intensification) → sets of 5.

## Key Concepts
- **Tempo running** — runs of ~100–200 yards/meters at a "stride" pace (between jog and sprint) with walking recovery; used for the mandatory 2–3 week base-building period before functional programming begins.
- **The 80-20 rule (Pareto principle) applied to strength training** — ~80% of weight-room programming is the same across sports (per Marco Cardinale: "Your sport is not different, you just think it is"); only the conditioning/energy-system side varies meaningfully by sport.
- **Progressive resistance** — adding load or reps virtually every week as the primary engine of strength gain; theoretically ~5 lb/week could compound to +260 lb/year (though most athletes plateau before that).
- **The Functional Movement Screen "adding strength to dysfunction" warning** (Gray Cook) — loading a movement pattern before it is mastered compounds the dysfunction rather than fixing it; "learn to move before you load."
- **Body-weight-first upper body pulling exception** — many athletes (especially beginners and some female athletes) cannot start upper-body pulling work at bodyweight resistance; machine or elastic-band assistance is acceptable here as the true starting point, not a shortcut.

## Mental Models
- "The job is not to write workouts but to watch workouts" (Boo Schexnayder) — a program on paper means nothing until you've watched the athlete execute it and decided baseline vs. regression.
- "Everything has to look good" — technique (assessed visually, the "eye test") always outranks the amount of weight lifted; if an exercise doesn't look smooth and athletic, regress it.
- Design the program for the athlete's needs, not the coach's personal preferences ("don't design a program based on what you like or dislike as a coach").
- A well-coached bad program beats a poorly coached good program every time — execution and coaching cues matter more than programming sophistication.

## Anti-patterns
- **Combining bits of multiple published programs as a beginner ("recipe mixing")**: Boyle repeatedly observes that athletes-turned-coaches who blend his program with others produce consistently worse results — "invariably the program is poor."
- **Loading an unmastered movement pattern**: The single biggest program-destroying mistake per Boyle — e.g., adding external load to a squat before bodyweight technique is competent.
- **Letting athletes self-select pace and rest during conditioning**: Athletes left to run "at their own pace" run too slowly; left to control their own rest, they rest too long. Conditioning work must be prescribed with explicit distance, pace, and rest.
- **Treating the stability ball as central to functional training**: Overuse of one tool (video of athletes standing on stability balls) discredited the entire field in many coaches' eyes; stability balls are appropriate only for a few specific exercises (rollouts, leg curls) and should never be used for standing work, or as a substitute bench for loaded pressing.
- **Applying lower expectations or strength-training taboos to female athletes**: The chapter explicitly rejects the idea that female athletes need different program content — "any differences really have no bearing on how a training program would be designed or applied."

**Equipment adaptations for female/young athletes (avoids oversized jumps in % load increase):**

| Equipment | Standard | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Olympic bars | 15, 25, 35 lb (7, 11, 16 kg) versions | Full-size lighter bars let lighter athletes look like everyone else in the weight room (psychological benefit) |
| Dumbbells | 2.5 lb (1 kg) increments up to 50 lb | 5 lb increments create oversized relative jumps (e.g., 30→40 lb = 33% jump) for lighter athletes |
| PlateMates | 1.25 lb (0.6 kg) magnets, match hexagonal/round to dumbbell style | Retrofits 5 lb-increment dumbbells to smaller jumps |
| Olympic plates | 1.25 lb versions where available | 45→50 lb is a 10% jump — too large for most progressions |
| Dip belts / weight belts | Custom-fit, sizes 24–28 for smaller waists | Standard belts fall off smaller athletes |

## Worked Example
**Progressive resistance in practice — Ed Lippie and Ben Bruno:**
Ed Lippie (former collegiate football player, MBSC model/coach) used the accumulation→intensification cycle described in this chapter to reach 3 chin-ups with 135 lb (60 kg) added — the best Boyle has seen at his facility. Ben Bruno (former MBSC employee) used the same simple periodization approach to progress to 305 lb (140 kg) for 5 reps in the rear-foot-elevated split squat. Both results came from the plain 8-10-12 bodyweight progression → external load → 8/3/5 periodization cycle, not from anything exotic — Boyle's evidence that "simple, well-coached" beats "complex."

**Bodyweight progressive-resistance template (from the text):**
- Week 1: 3×8
- Week 2: 3×10
- Week 3: 3×12
- Week 4: progress to a harder exercise variant, or add external load (dumbbell, kettlebell, vest, sandbag, medicine ball), then reapply 8-10-12 or the accumulation/intensification cycle.

## Key Takeaways
1. Know your proficiency level (cook/sous-chef/chef) and don't mix program sources beyond what that level supports.
2. Master bodyweight technique before adding any external load — the single biggest program-wrecking mistake is loading too soon.
3. Use the Baseline/Progression/Regression system: hold baseline ~3 weeks, progress on mastery (the "eye test"), regress immediately on struggle.
4. Rate exercises on the functional continuum (stability source → stance → joint count) to decide what belongs in a program.
5. Periodization can stay simple: alternate higher-volume (8–10) and higher-intensity (3–5) rep ranges; don't over-engineer it.
6. Prescribe conditioning explicitly (distance, pace, rest) — athletes left to self-regulate go too slow and rest too long.
7. Design programs for the athlete, not coach preference — and remember execution/coaching quality beats programming sophistication.
8. Female athletes need equipment accommodations (lighter bar/plate increments) and body-image-aware coaching, not different exercise selection or lowered expectations.
