# Chapter 3: Assessing Functional Strength

## Core Idea
After analyzing sport demands, assess strength using body-weight-relative, sport-realistic tests (chin-ups, push-ups, suspension rows, rear-foot-elevated split squats) rather than machine or supine 1RM numbers — because raw numbers without bodyweight context, and positions athletes never occupy in sport (like lying on the back), don't measure usable, functional strength.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Functional Upper Body Strength Battery**: Three tests Boyle considers the most effective and accurate.
  - When to use: Establishing a baseline before program design, and periodically to track strength progress.
  - How: (1) Max chin-ups/pull-ups (strict form, full elbow extension, chin above bar, no kipping); (2) Max suspension inverted rows (thumbs touch chest, body rigid and straight); (3) Max push-ups (chest to a 2-inch pad, rigid torso, paced at 50 bpm metronome). Progress each to weighted reps once 10 strict bodyweight reps are achieved.
- **Rear-Foot-Elevated Split Squat (RFESS) as functional lower body test**: A safer alternative to 1RM back squat testing.
  - When to use: Assessing lower-body functional strength when a safe, valid, reliable test is needed (back squat 1RM testing carries injury risk and reflects a football-specific tradition, not universal sport need).
  - How: Back foot elevated on a bench/stand, Airex pad under front knee, load selected in the 5RM range using dumbbells/kettlebells held at the sides (side-loading, not back/front-rack, for fail-safety), performed to technical failure.
- **Bodyweight-relative interpretation of numbers**: Raw strength numbers are meaningless without bodyweight context.
  - When to use: Any time a strength number (e.g., bench press max) is used to judge an athlete.
  - How: Ask what the number represents relative to the athlete's bodyweight, not the number in isolation — a 350 lb bench press from a 350 lb athlete is not impressive.

## Key Concepts
- **Functional strength** — strength an athlete can actually use in sport-relevant positions (standing, closed-chain, single-leg), as distinct from strength expressed in a position (e.g., supine bench press) that never occurs at a high level of sport performance.
- **RM (repetition maximum)** — the most reps an athlete can perform at a preselected load; used here instead of true 1RM testing for safety.
- **Kipping** — using body momentum to assist a pull-up/chin-up rep; explicitly disallowed in valid testing.
- **Technical failure** — the point at which an athlete can no longer maintain perfect exercise technique; the stopping criterion for RM-based lower-body testing (not just muscular failure).
- **"Develop great athletes that are good weightlifters"** (Denis Logan, EXOS) — Boyle's guiding quote for balancing traditional strength methods with functional methods rather than treating them as mutually exclusive.

## Mental Models
- Think of strength as "the road to speed and power" — no commentator says an athlete lost a play by being too fast or too strong, so strength deficits are rarely the limiting factor coaches should protect against overcorrecting.
- "If you're lying on your back pushing up, you stink at football" — use this as a gut check for whether a strength test position reflects real sport performance; it doesn't mean abandon the bench press, but don't let it be the sole strength metric.
- Tests are for evaluating progress, not the program itself — never let testing protocol dictate season-long training emphasis.

## Anti-patterns
- **Judging strength by raw numbers without bodyweight context**: A 350 lb bench press from a 350 lb athlete reflects bodyweight-equivalent strength, not elite strength.
- **Using only supine/machine strength tests (e.g., flat bench 1RM) as the sole upper-body assessment**: Doesn't reveal whether an athlete can perform sport-relevant closed-chain movements like push-ups and chin-ups; failing those despite a strong bench indicates a real injury-risk gap (especially shoulder).
- **1RM back squat testing for lower-body assessment**: Carries meaningful injury risk at true 1RM, especially for athletes untrained in the movement, and over-represents a football-specific training tradition.
- **Testing an exercise the athlete hasn't been taught**: If you can't safely teach the movement first, the informational value of the test doesn't outweigh the injury risk — don't test it.
- **Treating rowing/pulldown machine progressions as the fix for a failed chin-up test**: Improving chin-up performance requires assisted chin-ups or eccentric-emphasis chin-ups (10–20 sec lowers), not lat pulldowns.

## Reference Tables
**Upper body strength test progressions and elite benchmarks (per Boyle's program):**

| Test | Progression trigger | Elite benchmark examples |
|---|---|---|
| Chin-up/pull-up | Add 25 lb (10 kg) dip-belt weight once 10 strict bodyweight reps achieved | Females: 5 reps @ 45 lb (20 kg) added; Males: 90+ lb (40 kg) added; BU hockey team average 1RM chin-up ≈ 100 lb (45 kg) added |
| Suspension inverted row | Add 10 lb (5 kg) weight vest once 10 reps achieved | — |
| Push-up | Add 10 lb (5 kg) vest, then 20 lb (10 kg) vest, once 10 reps achieved; further load via plates on back | — |

**Lower body strength test benchmarks:**

| Test | Load / protocol | Elite benchmark |
|---|---|---|
| Rear-foot-elevated split squat | 5RM load, dumbbells/kettlebells at sides, to technical failure | — |
| Single-leg squat | Progress to this only after 3 weeks split squat + 3 weeks RFESS if unfamiliar with single-leg work | Athletes typically handle 5 reps @ 5 lb (2.5 kg) dumbbells by week 4 of a properly designed program; elite females: 10 reps @ 40 lb (20 kg) total load (20 lb vest + 10 lb dumbbells); elite males: up to 100 lb (45 kg) total external load |
| Double-leg vertical jump | Just Jump System or Vertec device | Used as leg-power proxy; retest after a strength block to confirm power gains track strength gains |

## Worked Example
**Boston University men's hockey — functional strength testing and injury reduction:**
Boyle instituted the functional strength battery from this chapter with the BU hockey team. Initially, 10–15 bodyweight chin-ups was considered excellent; five years later that same number was below average, with weighted chin-ups (some at 45 lb/20 kg plates) becoming the norm. A key downstream outcome: very few collision-related shoulder injuries occurred on BU teams during this period — offered as evidence that a training balance of pushing and pulling strength against resistance has real injury-prevention value, not just a performance benefit.

## Key Takeaways
1. Test functional (bodyweight-relative, closed-chain) strength, not just traditional 1RM lifts in isolation.
2. Chin-ups, suspension inverted rows, and push-ups (with strict form criteria) are Boyle's three core functional upper-body tests.
3. The rear-foot-elevated split squat, tested for reps at a 5RM load with side-loaded dumbbells/kettlebells, is a safer proxy for lower-body strength than 1RM back squat testing.
4. Never test a movement the athlete hasn't been safely taught first — the risk outweighs the informational benefit.
5. Progress bodyweight tests to weighted variants (dip-belt, weight vest) once an athlete clears ~10 strict reps, because the goal is strength development, not endurance.
6. Testing exists to evaluate progress and guide the plan — it is not the training program itself.
