## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**Functional training = purposeful, sports-general, standing, self-stabilized, multijoint training.** Use the **three questions test** (Ch 1) to evaluate any exercise: how many sports are played sitting, with external stability, or via single-joint isolation? Almost none — so default to standing, closed-chain, multijoint movement. The exception is the **"isolation for innervation"** paradox (Mark Verstegen): the deep abdominals, hip abductors/rotators, and scapular stabilizers often need brief isolated activation before they can function in compound movement.

**Sports-general beats sport-specific for strength.** Most sports share far more movement commonality than difference (Ch 1, 2, 11) — "your sport is not different, you just think it is" (Marco Cardinale). Build programs around common patterns (push, pull, knee-dominant, hip-dominant, core) rather than mimicking sport skills. The **80-20 rule** applies: ~80% of weight-room programming is identical across sports; only conditioning (energy-system matching) and overhead-athlete shoulder protection genuinely vary by sport.

**"Train slow, get slow."** Long, steady-state distance work degrades speed/power and carries high injury risk (~60% of new runners get hurt). Analyze sport demands by work:rest ratio and effort pattern (Ch 2), not total distance. Build conditioning bases through **tempo running → shuttle running**, never jogging (Ch 2, 11).

**The Joint-by-Joint Approach** (Gray Cook & Boyle, Ch 5): the body alternates between joints that need mobility and joints that need stability. Ankle→mobility, knee→stability, hip→mobility (and sometimes also stability), lumbar spine→stability, thoracic spine→mobility, scapula→stability, glenohumeral→mobility trained for stability. Dysfunction at one joint shows up as pain in the joint above or below — treat the joint below/above the pain site, not just the site itself.

**"Adding strength to dysfunction" is the cardinal program-design sin** (Gray Cook, applied throughout Ch 4, 6, 10). Never load a movement pattern the athlete hasn't mastered at bodyweight. Teach the pattern (mobility, positioning) separately from loading it. Use the **Baseline / Progression / Regression** system (Ch 4): baseline exercises held ~3 weeks, progressed on the coach's "eye test" (does it look smooth and athletic?), regressed immediately on technical struggle.

**Unilateral (single-leg) training is the priority for lower-body strength** (Ch 6), driven by the **bilateral limb deficit** — summed single-leg force output exceeds two-leg output — and by the fact that running/jumping happen on one leg. Split squats and single-leg straight-leg deadlifts are Boyle's two highest-priority lower-body exercises for any program. Squat depth is defined as **"powerlifting parallel"** (femur parallel to floor), never by knee angle, and never coached below parallel for general athletes.

**Core training is about motion prevention, not motion creation** (Ch 7). Train antiextension, antilateral flexion, and antirotation — never crunches, side bends, or trunk twists. Rotary power for striking/throwing sports comes from hip rotation with a stable lumbar spine (lumbar rotation ROM is only ~13° total and is "more dangerous than beneficial" per Sahrmann), trained via hip-driven medicine ball throws against a wall.

**Upper body training must balance pulling and pushing 1:1** (Ch 8) — imbalance (chasing "mirror muscles") is the primary driver of rotator cuff injury. Use the **push-pull ratio test**: chin-up-equivalent 1RM (bodyweight + added load) should be ≥ bench press 1RM.

**Plyometric progression is competence-gated, not time-gated** (Ch 9): Phase 1 (stable landing, eccentric strength — the most-skipped, most-important phase) must be mastered before Phases 2-4 (elastic, true reactive plyometrics). Balance jumps (2-leg), hops (1-leg, same-foot), and bounds (1-leg, opposite-foot) in multiple directions — most ACL injuries occur in single-leg, multidirectional situations that jump-only programs never train for.

**Olympic lifting is a tool (like a chainsaw), taught exclusively from the hang position** (bar above the knees, never the floor) (Ch 10). Skip it entirely if the coach isn't confident teaching it — substitute medicine balls, kettlebell swings, or plyometrics. Priority order for *why* to Olympic lift: athleticism/coordination first, eccentric strength (injury prevention) second, power output third.

**Program structure is universal; conditioning is what's actually sport-specific** (Ch 11). The same 9 program components and 4-phase periodization model (Poliquin undulating: accumulation → intensification → accumulation → strength-endurance) apply across sports. Training-day count (2/3/4-day), not sport, determines program structure. Conditioning must match each sport's real work:rest ratio, motor pattern, and directional demands — use Charlie Francis's **high-low model** (>90% or <80% effort, nothing between) to prevent overreach.
